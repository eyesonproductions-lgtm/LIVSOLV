Which one should I buy?

One refrigerator, shorter outage: the 1 kWh class is the cheaper, lighter place to start.

Refrigerator + freezer or longer outage: start around 2 kWh and make recharging part of the purchase.

Several days: stored capacity alone is not the solution. Solar, generator charging or another refill route becomes central.

If a storm is coming: triage the food before the lights go out

1. Decide what is most expensive or hardest to replace. Know which freezer contains the meat, prepared meals, breast milk, specialty foods or other items you would most hate to lose. That tells you which appliance gets backup power first.

2. Freeze what can safely be frozen now. Move suitable refrigerated meat, leftovers and other foods you will not use immediately into the freezer while grid power is reliable. Freeze water containers too, leaving expansion room.

3. Eat or cook the most vulnerable food before the storm. If you have refrigerated food that is already near the end of its safe storage life, use it before the outage instead of creating another item that must be protected afterward. Cooking food does not make it shelf-stable, so cooked leftovers still need safe refrigeration.

4. Build the first-day cooler before you need it. Identify the drinks and foods the household is likely to reach for first. When an outage becomes prolonged, those can move to a working cooler so people are not opening the refrigerator or freezer repeatedly.

5. Photograph the freezer. Take quick photos of shelves or make a paper inventory. You can decide what is inside without opening the door, and the record may also be useful if you later need to document a loss.

Hidden backup: make the cold you already have last longer

Turn empty freezer space into stored cold.Freeze water in sturdy containers, leaving room for expansion. A fuller freezer holds temperature longer, and the containers can become drinking water as they thaw.
Create one “working cooler.”If an outage will last, move the food you expect to use that day into a cooler with ice or frozen packs. Open the cooler, not the main freezer, all day.
Let temperature decide when you spend power.Keep an appliance thermometer in the freezer. Do not burn scarce generator fuel or battery capacity simply because the grid is down. Preserve the cold first, then power the appliance as needed while staying inside food-safety limits.
Group frozen food together.Cold food packed together holds cold better than widely spaced packages. If you know a storm is coming, freeze refrigerated meat, leftovers or other suitable food you will not use immediately.
Know where to get block or dry ice before everyone needs it.FoodSafety.gov recommends planning local sources in advance. Dry ice can substantially extend freezer time, but handle it correctly and never touch it with bare hands.
Extra insulation can help only while the appliance is OFF.People in long outages sometimes add blankets or sleeping bags around an unpowered chest freezer to slow heat gain. Never cover vents, compressor areas or an appliance while it is operating. Remove all added insulation before powering it again.

FoodSafety.gov says an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold about 4 hours, a full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours. Use actual temperature, not smell or taste, to make food-safety decisions.

Why the runtime range is so wide

A refrigerator does not draw its compressor wattage continuously. It cycles. Temperature, age, size, defrost cycles, ice makers and door openings change consumption. Check the yellow EnergyGuide label. Annual kWh ÷ 365 gives estimated daily kWh. Measuring the appliance for a normal day is better still.

Capacity and output answer different questions. Watt-hours tell you roughly how long the battery can supply energy. AC output and surge capability determine whether the station can successfully start and operate the compressor.

What real outages teach

Do not spend battery immediately just because the grid dropped. Closed refrigerator and freezer doors preserve cold. Use appliance thermometers and food-safety limits rather than anxiety.

Protect energy for more than food. Well pumps, communications and medical loads may also depend on electricity.

Test the real system. Plug in your actual refrigerator and freezer, confirm compressor startup, observe battery drain, then test the solar or generator recharge path too.

Redundancy wins. Battery power is quiet and indoor-safe. Solar can replenish it when weather cooperates. A correctly operated generator can carry heavier loads or refill batteries when sunlight is poor.

Simple runtime math

Use your appliance's average energy consumption, not just its compressor running watts. A practical first estimate is usable battery watt-hours divided by the appliance's average watts over time. Allow for inverter and system losses. For a more accurate household answer, measure your refrigerator for 24 hours.

Do not treat any generic refrigerator runtime as a promise. The estimates above deliberately use ranges because real refrigerators vary too much for a single honest number.
