Power · another backup path

Your car can be an emergency power source.

A pure sine wave inverter can turn 12-volt battery power into household AC power. It is a relatively inexpensive backup option for refrigeration, communications, lights and electronics, but the inverter does not create electricity. Your vehicle battery and alternator determine how much power is actually available.

The short answer: A 1,000-watt inverter can be a useful outage bridge. It can often run a refrigerator or freezer, router, laptops, lights, chargers and other modest loads, but not all at once. It is not a substitute for whole-home backup power.

What can you actually run?

Good candidates

  • Refrigerator or freezer, if startup surge stays within the inverter limit
  • Phones, tablets and laptops
  • Modem and Wi-Fi router
  • LED lighting
  • Television and small electronics
  • Battery chargers
  • Some CPAP and other medical devices, after checking the device's exact wattage and manufacturer requirements

Usually not the job for this

  • Central air conditioning
  • Electric water heater
  • Electric range or oven
  • Clothes dryer
  • Large electric space heater
  • Multiple high-wattage kitchen appliances at once

A microwave, toaster, coffee maker or hair dryer can consume most or all of a 1,000W inverter's capacity by itself.

The number on the inverter is not the number your car promises.

A 1,000W inverter can draw roughly 90 amps from a 12V system at a heavy load after conversion losses. The Renogy 1000W model is rated for 1,000W continuous output, 2,000W peak surge and greater than 90% nominal efficiency. That is why this class of inverter connects directly to the battery with heavy cables instead of relying on a cigarette-lighter outlet.

Inverter capacity ≠ guaranteed vehicle output.

Your alternator must also power the vehicle itself. Some vehicles can support a substantial inverter load while running; others cannot continuously supply the inverter's full rating. Check the vehicle's alternator capacity and leave adequate reserve for the vehicle. If you are unsure, use a smaller load rather than assuming the full inverter rating is available.

How to use it during an outage

  1. Park the vehicle completely outdoors, well away from doors, windows and vents.
  2. Turn the vehicle off before making battery connections unless the inverter manufacturer specifically directs otherwise.
  3. Connect the inverter directly to the 12V battery using the correct short, heavy-gauge cables, fuse/protection and connection procedure specified by the inverter manufacturer.
  4. Start with the inverter off. Make secure connections, then start the vehicle if sustained power is needed.
  5. Connect one important load at a time and watch for overload, low-voltage or temperature warnings.
  6. Prioritize refrigeration, communication, medical needs and lighting before convenience loads.
Carbon monoxide can kill. Never run a vehicle in a garage, carport or other enclosed or partially enclosed area, even with the garage door open. Keep exhaust away from the house and occupied areas.

Can you just leave the inverter in a closet?

Yes. Unlike a portable power station, the inverter itself does not contain a large rechargeable battery that needs to be kept at a storage charge. Store it disconnected, dry, clean and protected from extreme moisture, corrosion and physical damage. Keep the cables, instructions and any required fuse hardware with it so the system is complete when the power is out.

LivSolv maintenance plan: every six months, inspect the inverter, cables and terminals, connect it correctly and run a small test load. Also confirm that the vehicle battery is healthy. An inverter in perfect condition is not useful if the battery supplying it is failing.

Where this fits in a real backup plan

A car-and-inverter setup is best treated as a separate backup path, not your only power plan. It is inexpensive compared with a large power station or generator, has no large stored battery to maintain, and uses a vehicle you already own. Its disadvantages are fuel use, engine hours, carbon-monoxide risk, battery/alternator limitations and the fact that you may need the vehicle for transportation.

Best use: bridge an outage, protect food, keep communications alive, or provide a fallback when your primary power station, solar system or generator is unavailable.
