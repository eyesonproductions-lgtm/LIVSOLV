Their emergency plan belongs beside yours.

Pets need food, safe water, transport and care when normal routines stop. Include every animal, not just the one with a leash.

First stepsPet kitDifferent animalsUseful suppliesPaper plan

Give every animal a person and a destination.

Choose who gets each animal into a carrier, who carries food and medicines, and who can help if you are away. Keep carriers, harnesses and leads where you can reach them. Practice loading calmly before an emergency.

If you evacuate, take your pets. Confirm a destination that accepts the species and number of animals you have. Not every emergency shelter or hotel accepts pets; ask before travelling. Service animals have different access rules.

Write the vet, emergency clinic, animal-control contact and backup caregiver on paper. Keep current ID tags, microchip details and a photo of you with each animal. Store copies of veterinary and ownership records with the kit.

A useful pet kit, without a novelty shopping list.

Food, water and care

  • Usual food in sealed storage, bowls and can opener
  • Separate safe drinking water for every animal
  • Prescribed medicines and written care instructions
  • Carrier sized for the animal, bedding, lead or harness
  • Vet records, ID information and recent photos

Start with several days and build gradually. CDC’s pet-kit checklist recommends two weeks of food, water and applicable medicines. Arrange refills with the vet; do not change doses or give human medicines.

Waste and comfort

  • Cat litter and a portable litter tray
  • Dog-waste bags and a lead for supervised breaks
  • Paper towels, gloves and suitable cleaning supplies
  • Familiar blanket or toy
  • Written feeding, medication and handling routine

For water planning, measure normal use over several days and ask the vet about heat, size or medical needs. Add this to the household reserve rather than borrowing from the people’s allocation.

Use bottled or otherwise approved safe water for pets during an advisory. Under a boil-water advisory, boiled water must cool before use. Keep pets away from floodwater, sewage, spills and damaged electrical equipment.

A cat carrier is not a complete plan for every animal.

Dogs and cats

Keep doors and carriers secure when routines are disrupted. A cat needs litter access as well as a carrier. Check carrier and harness fit now. A familiar towel and predictable feeding routine can make transport easier.

Birds and backyard chickens

Include ventilated transport crates, usual feed, waterers and absorbent bedding. Confirm a destination that accepts poultry or birds; an ordinary pet shelter may not. Keep poultry separate from other flocks and protect them from predators and weather.

Reptiles and turtles

Ask an exotics vet for the species’ safe temperature range, transport enclosure and backup heating plan. Write it down. Choose suitable backup equipment before an outage; avoid improvised direct-contact heat that can burn an animal.

Fish and powered habitats

List each essential pump, filter and heater and its power needs. Plan battery aeration or suitable backup power. Ask your aquatic vet or qualified aquarium specialist what can safely stop and for how long; one plan will not fit every tank.

AVMA’s Save the Whole Family guide includes species-specific planning, including backyard poultry. FDA links to bird and aquarium emergency resources.

Buy only what your animal’s plan is missing.

A secure carrier, ordinary food and water storage, waste supplies and a working care plan matter more than a branded “survival kit.” Check dimensions, animal weight limits and ventilation. Try carriers before relying on them in an evacuation.
