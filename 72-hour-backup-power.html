How much backup power do I need for a 3-day outage?

For three days, the important question is not just how big a battery to buy. It is what needs to stay running and how you will put energy back into the system after the first day. For most households, a power station without a realistic recharge plan is only a temporary battery.

The short answer

Food only: roughly 2 kWh is a practical battery starting point, paired with enough solar or another recharge source to replace part of what you use each day.

Food + Wi-Fi + lights: start around 2 kWh and treat recharging as part of the system, not an optional accessory.

More household essentials: build layers. A generator can handle heavy work, a power station can handle quiet periods and sensitive electronics, and solar can provide renewable replenishment when conditions cooperate.

What people discover during real outages

Published specifications tell us what equipment can do. Outage reports tell us what people actually wish they had done differently. Across multi-day outage accounts and experienced preparedness communities, the same lessons recur:

Test under real load

A generator or battery that turns on is not the same as a system proven to run your refrigerator, well pump, communications gear or other priority loads. Practice before the outage.

Do not depend on one energy source

Fuel can become difficult to obtain, sunlight can disappear behind clouds, and batteries eventually empty. Multiple recharge paths are more resilient than one giant device.

Plan beyond electricity

When a well pump, stove, internet connection or cell tower depends on power, an electrical outage can become a water, cooking and communications outage too.

The part people miss: recharging

Decide what absolutely has to stay on. Refrigeration, medical equipment, communications and temperature safety generally outrank convenience loads.
Measure or test the real loads. Do not discover during the outage that a heater, pump or appliance draws far more than you assumed.
Give yourself more than one refill path when possible. Solar, generator charging, vehicle charging and restored grid power each have different failure modes.
Practice a no-grid day. A short home test exposes dead batteries, missing adapters, insufficient extension cords, unexpectedly high loads and habits that waste stored energy.

Solar ratings are maximums under suitable conditions, not guaranteed output. Clouds, shade, panel angle, season and daylight all matter.

Build the system in layers

For a longer outage, the strongest architecture is often not “generator versus battery versus solar.” It is using each where it is strongest: generator for high-output work and fuel-based replenishment, power stations for quiet indoor-safe stored electricity and sensitive loads, and solar for renewable recharging.
