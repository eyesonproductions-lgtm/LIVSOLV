How to build a Dakota fire hole

A Dakota fire hole is simply two openings connected underground: a larger fire/cooking hole and a smaller air-intake hole. The short tunnel feeds fresh air to the base of the fire.

The short version

1
Choose safe ground.

Use firm, bare mineral soil away from roots, peat, mulch, dry grass, structures and anything that can burn underground. Do not use this method during a burn ban or high fire danger.

2
Dig the main fire hole.

About 8–12 inches wide and 8–12 inches deep is plenty for a compact cooking fire.

3
Dig the air-intake hole on the upwind side.

That means the side the wind is coming from. Keep it close to the main hole. A 4–6 inch-wide intake is enough.

4
Connect them underground.

Make a short tunnel from the intake into the lower part of the fire hole. If the holes are placed close together, roughly 3–6 inches of tunnel is usually enough. The point is airflow, not a long passage.

5
Build a small fire at the bottom.

Start with tinder and kindling, then add small dry fuel. Keep the fire compact so air from the tunnel can reach it.

6
Cook without choking the fire.

Use a stable metal grate or other secure noncombustible support when possible. Leave space around the cookware so hot gases and smoke can escape.

Why the second hole matters: the intake gives outside air a direct path toward the base of the fire. That helps a small fire burn efficiently and makes the design useful in windy conditions.
Before you leave: drown the fire, stir the soil and drown it again. Keep checking until the fire hole and surrounding soil are completely cold. Underground roots can carry fire beyond what you can see.
Do I really need exact measurements?

No. The geometry matters more than precision. You need a main chamber large enough for a small fire, a smaller surface opening that can pull in outside air, and an underground connection that enters low enough to feed the fire. Keep the openings close enough that you are not digging an unnecessarily long tunnel.

When should I skip a Dakota fire hole?

Skip it in peat, duff, root-filled ground, loose soil that may collapse, areas with buried utilities, or anywhere open fires are restricted. A purpose-built outdoor stove is the better choice when the ground is questionable.
