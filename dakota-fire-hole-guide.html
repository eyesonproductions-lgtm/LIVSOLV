How to build a Dakota fire hole

One main fire hole. One smaller air-intake hole on the upwind side. Connect them underground so incoming air feeds the base of the fire.

1Pick the right ground.

Use bare mineral soil well away from roots, peat, mulch, dry grass, structures and underground fuels. Do not build one during a burn ban.

2Dig the main hole.

Make it roughly 8–12 inches across and about 8–12 inches deep. It only needs to hold a compact cooking fire, not a bonfire.

3Find the upwind side.

About a foot from the main hole, dig a smaller air-intake opening. Putting it upwind helps moving air feed the fire.

4Connect the holes.

Dig diagonally from the smaller intake toward the bottom of the main fire hole. The tunnel should open near the fire's base so fresh air reaches combustion.

5Build a small fire.

Start with tinder and kindling, then feed small dry sticks. The goal is a hot compact fire with good airflow, not a hole packed solid with wood.

6Support the pot safely.

Use a stable metal grate if you have one, or bridge the opening only when your cookware can sit securely without blocking all exhaust. Do not balance boiling water on questionable rocks.

Important: outdoor use only. Keep water or an appropriate extinguisher nearby. Avoid wet, porous or unknown rocks around high heat. When finished, drown and stir the fire, then verify the surrounding soil is cold. Underground roots can carry fire beyond the visible hole.

Why does the second hole matter?

The intake creates a direct air path toward the bottom of the fire. That can help a compact fire burn hotter and more efficiently than a poorly ventilated ground pit. Wind direction and soil geometry still matter, so this is not a magic smokeless stove.

What if my soil is rocky, rooted or very dry?

Do not force this design into unsafe ground. Use a purpose-built outdoor stove on a stable noncombustible surface instead. A Dakota fire hole is only useful when the soil and fire conditions make digging and extinguishing it safe.
