How to build a Dakota fire hole

This is not an oven. It is a small outdoor wood fire built below ground, with a second opening that feeds air to the bottom of the fire. It can concentrate heat under a pot while using relatively small pieces of dry wood.

The basic idea: Dig a main fire hole roughly 10 inches across and about 12 to 15 inches deep. Dig a smaller air-intake opening on the upwind side and connect it to the bottom of the fire chamber with a short tunnel. The rising hot air pulls fresh air through the tunnel and feeds the fire.

Build it in six steps

1
Choose a safe outdoor location.

Use firm mineral soil that will hold its shape. Do not dig into peat, duff, roots or other organic soil that can smolder underground. Avoid loose sand. Check local burn restrictions before lighting anything.

2
Dig the fire chamber.

Make the main opening roughly 8 to 10 inches across and about 12 to 15 inches deep. Save the soil so you can completely fill the hole afterward.

3
Make the air intake upwind.

Start a smaller opening about 6 to 10 inches from the main chamber on the side the breeze is coming from. Angle it downward toward the base of the fire chamber.

4
Connect the two openings.

Carefully create a roughly fist-size tunnel between the intake and the bottom of the fire chamber. The walls and tunnel must be stable enough that they will not collapse.

5
Build a small fire in the main hole.

Start with dry tinder and small sticks. Once the fire catches, the rising heat should draw fresh air through the intake. Feed it small dry wood rather than trying to build a large campfire underground.

6
Cook without choking the fire.

Support a pot above the main opening on a stable metal grate or other appropriate noncombustible support, leaving enough space around it for hot gases to escape. Do not seal the opening with the pot.

OUTDOOR USE ONLY. A Dakota fire hole still produces carbon monoxide, smoke, sparks and extreme heat. Do not build one inside a house, garage, carport, tent, camper or other enclosed or partially enclosed area. Do not use it where underground roots or organic material could carry a hidden fire. Keep water or another appropriate extinguishing method immediately available.

Why would I use this instead of a normal campfire?

It concentrates the heat. The fire burns directly beneath the cookware rather than heating a large open area.

It uses small fuel. Sticks and smaller pieces of dry wood can provide useful cooking heat.

It handles wind better. Much of the flame is protected below ground.

It is a fallback, not the first choice. If you already have a safe outdoor propane/butane stove or purpose-built rocket stove and fuel, those are much easier. This is the skill to know when ordinary cooking options are gone.

When you are finished

Extinguish the fire completely. Stir the remains and make sure there is no residual heat or smoldering material. Only after it is fully cold should you refill both openings with the saved soil and restore the site. Never bury a live fire.
