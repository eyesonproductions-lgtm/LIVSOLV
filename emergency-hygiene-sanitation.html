Start with hands, a toilet plan and a way to wash.

A shower is a comfort. Clean hands and contained waste are the first priorities. Put soap, a water jug, a catch basin, toilet paper, bags and clean towels together before spending on new equipment.

If this is happening now: check your utility and local health department’s water and sewer instructions. Stop flushing if there is sewage backup, damaged plumbing, a flooded septic system or an instruction not to use the sewer. Never bathe in floodwater.

A water jug can replace a faucet.

Use a jug with a controllable spout, soap, clean towels and a basin to catch used water. Let clean water flow over hands; do not share a bowl of used wash water. Put the station near the toilet, away from food preparation.

Wash with soap and water for at least 20 seconds after toilet use, diaper changes and handling waste, and before food preparation or eating. Without soap and water, use hand sanitizer containing at least 60% alcohol. It works less well on dirty or greasy hands and does not remove every germ or chemical.

Body wipes are not a handwashing substitute. Surface-disinfecting wipes are not skin wipes. Never use household disinfectants on your body. CDC explains the difference.

Use what you own

Basin, cloth and towel

A seated wash with a small amount of safe water may be enough. Wash cleaner areas first and the toilet area last, using a fresh cloth as needed. Dry skin folds and change into clean underwear and socks. Follow the cleanser’s rinse instructions.

Keep bathroom cloths separate from kitchen cloths. Use the person’s usual tolerated products; emergency preparation is not the time to test strongly scented supplies.

Solar shower reality check

Sun warms the water; it does not disinfect it. Clouds, cold weather and short days limit heating. Test the water on your hand before use. A full 2.5-gallon bag holds about 21 pounds of water; five gallons is about 42 pounds, before the bag’s weight. Use a support rated for the whole load, not a towel rail or an untested shower rod.

If lifting or standing is difficult, a ground-level container with a rechargeable shower pump may be easier, but it needs a charged battery and safe bathing water. Never pour boiling water into a shower bag. Use a stable, nonslip washing area with privacy and drainage approved for the situation.

When the toilet cannot flush.

A power outage alone does not tell you whether flushing is safe. Gravity sewers may still operate, while sewage pumps and some septic systems need electricity. If only the water supply is off and the utility confirms the waste system is usable, follow utility and toilet-maker instructions for manual flushing. Otherwise, contain waste.

Simple portable toilet

Use a stable toilet with compatible human-waste bags, toilet paper, gloves and nearby handwashing. Follow bag limits; replace before it is heavy or at risk of leaking. Close and store used bags in a separate, secure container away from children, pets, food and water.

Toilet specifications · Compatible bags

Low-cost two-bucket backup

The twin-bucket method separates urine and solids using clearly marked buckets, a stable seat, liners and dry cover material. Use the regional emergency toilet guide for setup, then follow your own health department’s waste-storage and collection directions.

A lid is not a disposal plan. Do not assume human waste can go in normal trash. Never empty it into a storm drain, stream or garden, and do not bury it unless local authorities specifically permit and explain that method.

Keep all wipes, diapers, pads, tampons and waste bags out of the toilet, including wipes sold as “flushable.” Do not add bleach or mix cleaners into collected waste. For reusable chemical toilets, use only the maker’s treatment and an authorized emptying point.

Guidance: CDC septic precautions and OSU emergency sanitation. Local disposal instructions take priority.

“Not drinking water” does not always mean “safe shower water.”

  • Boil-water advisory: use bottled or appropriately boiled water for teeth. Bathing may be allowed with precautions; follow local instructions and keep children from swallowing it.
  • Do-not-drink advisory: chemicals may be involved. Boiling does not make chemical contamination safe. Read the specific instructions for bathing.
  • Do-not-use advisory: do not use tap water on skin or for other purposes while the advisory remains in effect.

Keep safe drinking water separate from washing water. Never use a container that held toxic chemicals. Capture wash water when drains are unusable and ask the utility how to dispose of it. See our water-storage and water-treatment guides.

The small supplies people actually miss.

Daily essentials

  • Soap, jug with spout, catch basin and towels
  • Toothbrushes, toothpaste and safe water
  • No-rinse body cloths or bath sponges
  • Toilet paper, waste bags and gloves
  • Clean underwear, socks and a separate dirty-laundry bag

Build around your household

  • Usual menstrual supplies and disposal bags
  • Diapers, incontinence products and changing supplies
  • Established skin-care or medical hygiene supplies
  • Seated washing and a safely rated toilet if needed
  • Pet waste supplies, kept separate from human hygiene gear

Count actual daily use × planned days × people who need that item, then add a modest buffer. A pack of eight cloths is not automatically eight full baths: check its directions. Wash clothes and towels before a forecast storm. Choose disposable menstrual or continence supplies if safe washing for reusables will be difficult.
