Medical preparedness without pretending you are a doctor

If someone gets hurt and help is delayed, what should I have at home?

A good home first-aid setup helps with minor problems and helps you support someone while professional care is being reached. It does not turn a serious injury into a do-it-yourself medical procedure.

Fast answer: Buy one organized kit you can actually understand, then add the few things inexpensive kits often lack. Do not choose by “300 pieces.” Choose by useful dressings, gauze, tape, gloves, tools, instructions and supplies matched to your household.

Two complete kits worth comparing

Best value starting point: Adventure Medical Kits Sportsman 200

Despite the outdoors name, this is an unusually sensible home starter because the supplies are organized by injury instead of dumped into one pouch. The current kit includes a 5×9 trauma pad, sterile dressings, gauze, cloth tape, EMT shears, gloves, wound irrigation syringe, butterfly closures, medications and a substantial first-aid reference book. The manufacturer rates it for up to four people for four days.

Why we like it: around the $50 class at the time of review, useful organization, fewer filler pieces, and an actual reference book for situations where the internet is unavailable.

What it lacks: it is still a basic first-aid kit, not a comprehensive home trauma setup. It has limited quantities of larger dressings and gloves and should be customized for your household.

More complete upgrade: MyMedic MyFAK

The MyFAK costs substantially more, but the current standard kit is broader: saline wash, cold pack, gloves, space blanket, multiple gauze and non-adherent dressings, burn supplies, medication modules, CPR shield, splint supplies, wound-closure supplies, EMT shears, thermometer and other tools. The current listed price is about $170 before promotions.

Why spend more: better organization and a much wider range of useful categories in one grab-and-go package.

Why not: it is expensive, some contents may be unnecessary for your household, and buying a premium kit does not substitute for first-aid training. Certain Pro configurations add advanced trauma items that should only be used by someone who knows how to use them.

If you want one more comprehensive bag and the price is comfortable, the MyFAK is the cleaner upgrade path.

One of the first upgrades we would add: adjustable skin closures

Clozex Emergency Laceration Closures

This is the product category that looks a little like tiny adjustable ties across a cut. Adhesive pads sit on opposite sides of an appropriate wound and the closure mechanism draws the edges together without needles. It is materially different from an ordinary butterfly strip.

Why it stands out: it solves a specific gap that many ordinary first-aid kits barely address. FDA has an OTC classification for adjustable adhesive closure devices intended for superficial minor cuts and lacerations.

Important limit: this is not something to slap over uncontrolled major bleeding, a deep wound, major contamination, a bite, puncture, embedded object or burn. Those situations may require professional medical care. Follow the exact product labeling.

LivSolv rating: one of our highest-priority upgrades to research when improving a basic home kit because it adds a capability ordinary adhesive bandages do not. We are not claiming personal testing of Clozex unless and until that exact product is personally tested.

What we would add even if you buy a kit

More 4×4 sterile gauze and at least a couple larger absorbent/trauma dressings. These are more useful for larger wounds than another hundred tiny adhesive bandages.

Good cloth medical tape. Gauze becomes much more useful when you can secure it reliably.

Extra nonlatex gloves. Two pairs disappear quickly if more than one person is helping or the first pair is contaminated or damaged.

Quality trauma/utility shears. Useful for safely cutting clothing or dressings when access matters.

Instant cold packs and an emergency blanket. Both are compact and remain useful when electricity is unavailable.

A real thermometer. Objective information is more useful than guessing.

Printed first-aid instructions. A QR code is not much help when the internet and cell network are down.

Household-specific supplies. Prescribed medications, allergy information, spare glasses, hearing-aid batteries and other individual needs belong with the plan even though they cannot be standardized into a commercial kit.

Bleeding-control equipment deserves training, not just a shopping link. Current AHA/Red Cross guidance recognizes manufactured tourniquets, hemostatic agents and other bleeding-control supplies as potential first-aid-kit additions. LivSolv can recommend credible equipment, but we will not imply that owning it teaches someone how or when to use it.
I have $0 to spend. What useful things are probably already in my house?

Clean towels or cloth can provide material for direct pressure when commercial gauze is unavailable. A clean plastic bag can provide some barrier protection if gloves are unavailable. A wrapped bag of ice or frozen food can function as a cold pack for appropriate minor injuries. Soap and clean running water are useful for basic hygiene and cleaning many minor wounds. Keep existing medications together, write medications/allergies/conditions and emergency contacts on paper, and save reputable first-aid instructions where they remain accessible offline.

Improvisation has limits. LivSolv will not recommend homemade medicines, DIY suturing, improvised invasive procedures or sharing prescription medication.

When this stops being a first-aid-kit problem

Life-threatening bleeding, breathing problems, loss of consciousness, signs of stroke, severe chest pain, major burns, severe allergic reactions, significant head/neck/spine injuries and other potentially life-threatening conditions require emergency medical care.

If normal communications are disrupted, use every available route to reach professional help rather than escalating into procedures beyond your training.
