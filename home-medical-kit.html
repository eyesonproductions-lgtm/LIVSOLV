Medical preparedness without pretending you are a doctor

If someone gets hurt and help is delayed, what should I have at home?

A good home first-aid setup helps you handle minor problems and support someone while professional help is on the way. It does not turn a serious injury into a do-it-yourself medical procedure.

Fast answer: Start with a complete first-aid kit, then add the few things inexpensive kits often lack: enough gauze and dressings, good tape, gloves, a thermometer, cold packs, an emergency blanket, a recognized bleeding-control option if you are trained to use it, and household-specific medications and medical information.

Two ways to start

Simple ready-made kit: American Red Cross 42-Piece Personal First Aid Kit

This is a straightforward baseline rather than a giant “300-piece” bag padded with hundreds of tiny bandages. The current Red Cross kit includes gauze, a trauma pad, roller bandage, gloves, tape, scissors, antiseptic supplies and a first-aid guide.

Best for: someone who wants a small, credible starter kit and will add household-specific supplies.

Build or upgrade a family kit instead

The Red Cross family-of-four checklist is a useful reality check for any premade kit. It calls for compress dressings, assorted bandages, cloth tape, gauze rolls and pads, gloves, cold compress, emergency blanket, thermometer, triangular bandages, tweezers and first-aid instructions, plus personal medications and emergency information.

LivSolv rule: compare what is actually inside a kit, not the giant piece count printed on the bag.

The unusual add-on worth knowing about: adjustable skin closures

The “little zip ties over a cut” product category is real.

Devices such as Clozex use adhesive pads and an adjustable closing mechanism to bring the edges of an appropriate laceration together without needles. This category is materially different from an ordinary butterfly strip.

But this distinction matters: FDA's OTC adjustable-closure classification is for superficial minor cuts and lacerations and excludes deep wounds and burns. A deep, gaping, contaminated, puncture, bite, embedded-object or heavily bleeding wound is not something LivSolv is going to tell readers to simply zip closed at home.

There is also a regulatory nuance: OSHA treats devices such as Clozex/ZipStitch as medical treatment rather than ordinary first aid for workplace recordkeeping, even though adjustable closure devices can be cleared for OTC use. So we will describe exactly what a specific product is cleared and labeled for instead of calling every closure strip “first aid.”

Important: an adjustable skin closure is not a substitute for bleeding control. For life-threatening external bleeding, Red Cross guidance prioritizes emergency help and firm direct pressure, with a tourniquet or wound packing when appropriate and the responder is trained. Do not close a wound over uncontrolled major bleeding.

Specific items that can matter more than another box of Band-Aids

Absorbent compress/trauma dressings: useful for applying pressure and covering larger wounds.

Sterile gauze pads and rolls: versatile and worth having in useful quantities.

Good cloth medical tape: makes the gauze substantially more useful.

Nonlatex disposable gloves: basic barrier protection when dealing with blood or body fluids.

Instant cold packs: useful when electricity or freezer access is unavailable.

Emergency blanket: tiny, inexpensive and included in the Red Cross family-kit recommendation.

Thermometer: gives you actual information instead of guessing whether someone has a fever.

First-aid instructions: arguably more important than another gadget. Keep instructions accessible even when internet service is down.

I have $0 to spend. What useful things are probably already in my house?

Clean towels or cloth can provide material for direct pressure when commercial gauze is unavailable. A clean plastic bag can provide some barrier protection if gloves are unavailable. A wrapped bag of ice or frozen food can function as a cold pack for appropriate minor injuries. Soap and clean running water are useful for basic hygiene and cleaning many minor wounds. Keep your existing medications together, write down medications/allergies/conditions and emergency contacts on paper, and download or print reputable first-aid instructions before an outage.

Improvisation has limits. LivSolv will not recommend homemade medicines, DIY suturing, improvised invasive procedures or sharing prescription medication.

When this stops being a first-aid-kit problem

Life-threatening bleeding, breathing problems, loss of consciousness, signs of stroke, severe chest pain, major burns, severe allergic reactions, significant head/neck/spine injuries and other potentially life-threatening conditions require emergency medical care.

If normal communications are disrupted, use every available route to reach professional help rather than escalating into procedures beyond your training.
