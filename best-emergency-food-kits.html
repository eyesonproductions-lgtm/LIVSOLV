Don't compare kits by “servings” alone.

Emergency-food companies do not all define a serving the same way. A giant serving count can look impressive while providing less actual food than you expected.

Look at calories per person per day.

Then look at how much water the food requires, whether you need a way to heat it, how long it stores and whether the people in your house will actually eat it.

The catch nobody should hide: these meals need water.

Most freeze-dried emergency meals need water for preparation. That means buying a month of emergency food without planning your emergency water supply creates another problem.

Before buying a large food kit, make sure your water plan can support it.

Do you even need emergency food kits?

No.

For many households, a rotating pantry of ordinary shelf-stable food is cheaper and more useful. You buy extra food you already eat, use the oldest items first and replace them as you go.

Emergency kits become useful when you specifically value long shelf life, compact storage and convenience.

Don't spend money because preparedness marketing scared you.

If ordinary grocery food solves your problem, buy ordinary grocery food. If long-term emergency food makes your life easier, buy that instead.
