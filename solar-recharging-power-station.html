Solar recharging without the solar-engineering lesson

How much solar do I need to recharge a power station?

The simple answer: match the solar setup to the size of your battery and how quickly you need to refill it. A giant battery with one tiny panel can work, but you may wait a very long time.

The short answer

About a 1 kWh power station: 400–800W of solar is a practical range when you want meaningful daytime recharging.

About a 2 kWh power station: 800–1,200W is a much better fit for outage use, if the power station can actually accept that much solar.

For multi-day resilience: buy the battery and the recharge system together. Solar-panel wattage only helps up to the station's solar-input limit.

Pick the situation that sounds like yours

I just want some solar backup

You mostly expect grid charging but want a way to refill during a longer outage.

Start around 400W

I want solar to matter

You want to replace a meaningful chunk of the energy you use each day.

Look around 800W

I am planning for several days

You want solar to be a central part of the backup system, especially with a 2 kWh battery.

Aim toward 1,000–1,200W

One rule that saves people money: check the power station's maximum solar input before buying panels. Putting 1,200W of panels on a station that only accepts 400W does not magically make it charge three times faster.

Why panel wattage is not the same as real charging power

The number on the panel is a laboratory rating. Real output changes with sun angle, cloud cover, heat, shade and how the panel is positioned.
Your power station imposes its own ceiling. Its voltage, current and wattage limits determine what it can actually take from the panels.
You still need enough daylight. Even a well-sized solar array can have a poor day in heavy clouds or shade, so multi-day backup should not depend on a perfect forecast.

So what would we choose?

If the goal is a serious multi-day outage setup, we would rather pair a roughly 2 kWh battery with around 800–1,200W of usable solar input than buy a large battery that can only sip power back in slowly. For a smaller, easier-to-move system, the EcoFlow DELTA 3 Plus is interesting because its solar-input capacity is nearly as large as its battery capacity.

Do not buy panels by wattage alone. Connector type, open-circuit voltage, current limits and series/parallel configuration must be compatible with the specific power station. When we recommend complete solar kits later, compatibility will be part of the recommendation, not something we expect you to figure out afterward.
