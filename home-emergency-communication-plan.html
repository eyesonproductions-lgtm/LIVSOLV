WHEN HOME LOSES ITS CONNECTIONS

Power, internet and cell service are down. How do I communicate?

Do not build the plan around one phone or one radio. Regional outages can overload or disable cell service at the same time household internet disappears. Use communication layers that fail differently.

Simple layered setup: weather/AM/FM radio for information in, FRS radios for nearby direct communication, printed contacts and rendezvous instructions when devices fail, and satellite messaging when you need an independent path beyond the affected area.

Make the family plan before buying gear

FEMA recommends deciding in advance how household members will receive alerts, where they will shelter, how they will evacuate, how they will reconnect if separated and whether the emergency kit needs updating. Your plan should also account for children, pets, medications and other household-specific needs.

Pick two meeting places: one close to home for a house-level emergency and another outside the immediate neighborhood if you cannot return home. Choose one out-of-area person everyone can contact. Put the person's number, both meeting places and critical household numbers on paper.

Use text when networks are strained. A text may get through when a voice call does not. Set up a household group text now, but keep the paper plan because the phone itself can fail.

Practice it. Every household member should know where to go and whom to contact without needing to open this webpage.

1. Information in

Keep a weather-capable radio that does not depend entirely on household power. A S.A.M.E.-capable NOAA alert radio can wake or alert you for programmed local hazards. A rechargeable crank/solar radio can provide a second long-outage path for NOAA and broadcast information.

NOAA Weather Radio reception is typically around 40 miles from a transmitter, but terrain and structures matter. Program and test your receiver at home before you need it.

Simple home pick: Midland WR120B. It is a dedicated NOAA alert radio with localized programming rather than another gadget pretending to do ten jobs. For a portable backup, a quality rechargeable AM/FM/NOAA radio can complement it. Start rechargeable radios fully charged. Treat hand crank and tiny integrated solar panels as backup charging methods, not primary power sources.

2. Nearby people

FRS radios are the simplest license-free family layer in the U.S. They work directly without a cell tower or Wi-Fi. Give them to the people or nearby households you actually expect to coordinate with and agree on a channel beforehand.

Do not buy by the mileage number on the package. Buildings, hills, trees and antenna height can reduce usable range dramatically. Test the actual route between home, school, work or nearby family instead of trusting an advertised maximum.

Rocky Talkie Mountain Radio is a strong simple FRS option. It is license-free, transmits at the 2-watt FRS maximum and the manufacturer reports 1–5 miles as a more typical backcountry range even though ideal line-of-sight range can be much farther. That realistic range language is one reason we prefer it over radios marketed around fantasy mileage claims.

If FRS does not cover the area you actually need, GMRS can be the next practical step for many U.S. families because it can support more capable equipment and repeaters. It has different FCC licensing requirements, so treat it as a system you learn and test rather than a magic longer-range walkie-talkie.

3. Outside the affected area

Satellite communication is the independent long-distance layer for ordinary households. A two-way satellite communicator can provide messaging and SOS beyond a local network failure, subject to sky view, device capability, satellite availability and an appropriate active service plan.

Best-value simple option: ZOLEO. It provides two-way satellite messaging, check-ins and SOS and can send a check-in or SOS directly from the device even if the paired phone is unavailable. An active service plan is required for satellite service.

Higher-capability option: Garmin inReach Messenger Plus. It adds photo and voice messaging through the Garmin Messenger app when paired with a compatible smartphone, in addition to satellite text/SOS capability. It also requires an active satellite subscription.

4. Ham radio: powerful, but only if someone learns it

Amateur radio can provide capabilities that simple consumer radios cannot, including access to repeaters and, with the right equipment, skill and conditions, much longer-distance communication. But buying a handheld does not create an emergency communication system by itself.

Practical recommendation: if ham interests you, get licensed, practice during normal life and learn what works from your actual location. For a household that does not want a radio hobby, FRS/GMRS for local coordination plus satellite messaging for long distance is usually a more approachable architecture.

5. The layer that needs no battery

Keep a printed household communication card with names, phone numbers, addresses, an out-of-area contact, both meeting locations, school/work contacts, medical/emergency contacts, radio channel and basic instructions. Give a copy to every household member and keep another in the home emergency kit.

For children or anyone who may be separated from the household, make the instructions simple enough to use under stress. FEMA provides a fillable family communication plan and a kids' version if you want a ready-made template.

Before communications fail

Turn on emergency alerts. Keep Wireless Emergency Alerts enabled and sign up for relevant local alerts. FEMA recommends having multiple ways to receive warnings rather than relying on a single app or device.

Test every device and every power method. Charge radios and power banks. Store correct spare batteries where applicable.

Test the radio route. Walk or drive the actual neighborhood route and learn where coverage fails. If another household is part of your plan, test from both homes.

Activate satellite equipment beforehand. Do not discover during an outage that the service is inactive or unfamiliar.

Preserve phone power. Even without service, a phone can retain offline maps, contacts, notes, photos and downloaded information. Use self-powered radios for routine listening when possible.

Run one short drill. Separate for a few minutes, follow the contact and meeting instructions, then fix whatever was confusing.

Easy memory:
NOAA/AM/FM = information in.
FRS or GMRS = nearby group communication.
Satellite = outside the local failure.
Ham = powerful learned capability.
Paper plan = still works when batteries and networks do not.
