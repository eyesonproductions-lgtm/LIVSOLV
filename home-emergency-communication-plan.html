WHEN HOME LOSES ITS CONNECTIONS

Power, internet and cell service are down. How do I communicate?

Do not build the plan around one phone or one radio. Regional outages can overload or disable cell service at the same time household internet disappears. Use communication layers that fail differently.

Simple layered setup: weather/AM/FM radio for information in, FRS radios for nearby direct communication, printed contacts and rendezvous instructions when devices fail, and satellite messaging when you need an independent path beyond the affected area.

What real outages teach

A recent five-day outage report described cell towers being unavailable or overwhelmed for the first two days. Other outage accounts describe losing internet, cell boosters and even local radio at the same time. That means “everyone has a phone” is not a communication plan.

Practical consequence: decide where household members meet, identify an out-of-area contact, write critical numbers on paper, and establish the radio channel before the emergency. Technology should support the plan, not be the plan.

1. Information in

Keep a weather-capable radio that does not depend entirely on household power. A dedicated S.A.M.E.-capable alert radio is useful for automatic local alerts. A rechargeable crank/solar radio provides a second long-outage path for NOAA and broadcast information.

Current examples include Midland's emergency-radio line and C. Crane's CCRadio Solar. Start rechargeable radios fully charged. Treat hand crank and small integrated solar panels as endurance tools, not effortless primary charging.

Find the transmitter that serves your home while internet is available, then label the radio with the primary and a usable backup frequency. A normal AM/FM-only radio cannot receive NOAA Weather Radio.

2. Nearby people

FRS radios work directly without a cell tower or Wi-Fi. Give them to the people or nearby households you actually expect to coordinate with and agree on a channel beforehand.

Ignore giant package mileage claims. Buildings, hills and trees can reduce usable range dramatically. Test your actual route from home to the places that matter.

3. Outside the affected area

Satellite communication is the independent layer. A two-way satellite communicator can provide messaging and SOS beyond a local network failure, subject to sky view, device capability and an appropriate active service plan.

4. The layer that needs no battery

Keep a printed household communication card with names, phone numbers, addresses, an out-of-area contact, meeting locations, medical/emergency contacts, radio channel and basic instructions. Give copies to household members.

For children or anyone who may be separated from the household, make the instructions simple enough to use under stress.

Before communications fail

Test every device and every power method. Charge radios and power banks. Store correct spare batteries where applicable.

Test the FRS route. Walk or drive the actual neighborhood route and learn where radio coverage fails.

Activate satellite equipment beforehand. Do not discover during an outage that the service is inactive or unfamiliar.

Preserve phone power. Even without service, a phone can retain maps, contacts, notes, photos and offline information. Use self-powered radios for routine listening when possible.

Practice once. A short family drill reveals whether anybody actually remembers the channel, meeting point or out-of-area contact.

Easy memory:
Radio = information in.
FRS = nearby direct talk.
Satellite = outside the local failure.
Paper plan = still works when batteries and networks do not.
