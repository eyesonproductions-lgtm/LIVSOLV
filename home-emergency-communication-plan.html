When home loses its connections

The storm knocked everything out. How do I communicate from home?

If the power, internet and cell network all fail, your phone may stop being your connection to the outside world. The solution is not one magic radio. It is a few simple layers that do different jobs.

The simple home setup: Keep a NOAA weather radio for official information, ideally one that does not depend on household power. Use FRS radios if you need to talk directly with nearby family or neighbors. If you need a way to message people beyond the local outage, add a satellite communicator.

1. First, make sure information can still reach you

Keep a NOAA Weather Radio at home.

NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts official warnings, watches, forecasts and other hazard information 24/7. There are two useful ways to cover this job.

Option A: automatic alerts. A S.A.M.E.-capable alert radio can be programmed for your area and can sound alerts even when you are not actively listening. If you choose a desktop model, make sure it has working battery backup so a power outage does not take the radio with it.

Option B: hand-crank emergency radio. For a long outage, a portable NOAA radio with several ways to power itself is an excellent second option. The Midland ER310 can run from its rechargeable battery and can be recharged by solar or hand crank. The current C. Crane CCRadio Solar can use its rechargeable battery, hand crank, solar charging or AA batteries. That means you are not forced to spend your phone power bank just to hear weather information.

The tradeoff: do not assume every crank radio provides the same automatic county-specific alert behavior as a dedicated S.A.M.E. desktop radio. The strongest setup can be a dedicated alert radio for automatic warnings plus a crank/solar radio as the independent long-outage backup.

These are receive-only. They tell you what is happening. They do not let you talk back.

Quick reference: what do I tune the weather radio to?

If your radio has a WEATHER, WX or WB switch/button: select that mode first. Many emergency radios step automatically through the seven NOAA Weather Radio channels. Stop on the channel with the clearest continuous weather broadcast for your area.

If your radio has a tuning dial or displays the actual frequency: NOAA Weather Radio uses only these seven frequencies in the United States:

162.400 MHz
162.425 MHz
162.450 MHz
162.475 MHz
162.500 MHz
162.525 MHz
162.550 MHz

Start at 162.400 and work upward until you hear a clear NOAA broadcast. The best frequency depends on which transmitter serves your location, not simply which state you live in. Terrain can also make a slightly farther transmitter come in better than the closest one.

Important: an ordinary AM/FM-only radio cannot receive these NOAA frequencies. The radio must have a weather band, often marked WX, WB, Weather or NOAA.

Do this while the internet still works: find the NOAA transmitter that serves your home, write its frequency and call sign on a piece of tape or label, and stick it directly on the radio. Also write down one backup NOAA frequency that you can receive. If you have a S.A.M.E. alert radio, save your county code in the radio before the emergency.

2. If family is nearby, have a direct way to talk

FRS radios work without cell towers, Wi-Fi or a subscription.

Give radios to the people or households you actually expect to coordinate with and agree on a channel ahead of time. In the U.S., compliant FRS radios do not require an individual FCC license.

Do not buy based on the giant mileage number on the package. Hills, trees, buildings and terrain can dramatically reduce range. Real neighborhood range can be a fraction of the advertised line-of-sight maximum.

3. If you need to reach outside the outage

Satellite communication is the independent layer.

A satellite communicator such as Garmin inReach can send and receive messages through the Iridium satellite network rather than relying on a local cell tower. Satellite features require an active service plan and the device needs suitable access to the sky.

This can be useful at home after a regional communications failure, not just while hiking or driving somewhere remote.

Easy way to remember it:
Weather radio = information in.
FRS = nearby people talking directly.
Satellite = messaging beyond the local outage.

Do this before the storm

Test the weather radio and know every way it can be powered. If it accepts replaceable batteries, keep the correct spares with it.

If you own a crank radio, practice using it now. A crank is an emergency charging method, not unlimited effortless electricity. Start an outage with the radio charged and use the crank or solar input to extend your independence.

Charge handheld radios and store spare batteries if the model accepts them.

Choose the FRS channel ahead of time with the people you expect to contact. A radio in your house does nothing for a relative who does not have the matching plan.

Activate and test satellite equipment before an emergency. Do not leave first-time setup for the moment the cell network is already gone.

Save your phone power bank for jobs that actually need it. Even without cell service, the phone can still be useful for stored contacts, notes, maps and pairing with compatible satellite devices. A self-powered weather radio keeps routine listening from competing for that limited battery.

LivSolv rule: build communication in layers. You do not need satellite service just to hear tornado warnings, and a pair of walkie-talkies is not a substitute for a way to reach outside a widespread outage.
