PHONES + INTERNET ARE DOWN

How do I still get information and reach people?

You do not need every kind of radio. Start with a free family plan, then add only the communication layers your household actually needs.

Do this for free today: choose one meeting place near home and one outside your neighborhood, choose one out-of-area contact, create a family group text, turn on emergency alerts, and write the important numbers and meeting places on paper.
Why these free steps matter

During a regional emergency, calls may fail or networks may be overloaded. Texts can sometimes get through when calls do not. A paper plan still works if a phone is dead, lost or inaccessible. Everyone should know where to meet and who the out-of-area contact is before an emergency starts.

How far does NOAA reach?

NOAA says reception is typically around 40 miles from a transmitter, but mountains, buildings and terrain can interfere. Test the radio at your house before an emergency.

2. Talk to family or neighbors nearby

Rocky Talkie Mountain Radio

Who are you talking to? Another person who also has a compatible FRS radio. That might be your partner, child, neighbor, another car in your group or family staying nearby. You generally need at least two radios unless the person you plan to contact already owns one.

This is not a way to call 911 or automatically reach emergency services. It is direct radio-to-radio communication between people on compatible channels.

Range warning: mountains, hills, dense trees, concrete buildings and other obstructions can dramatically reduce range. Do not plan around the maximum mileage printed on a box. Test the exact places you care about.

What if I need more local range?

GMRS can add more capable radios and repeater access, but it has FCC licensing requirements. It makes sense when your family is willing to learn and test a local radio system rather than simply buying walkie-talkies.

3. Reach someone far away when local networks fail

This is where a satellite communicator becomes genuinely different. It does not need a nearby cell tower to send a satellite message, although it does need an appropriate view of the sky and an active plan.

Best value + easiest interface

ZOLEO

Pair it with your phone and use the ZOLEO app for familiar two-way messaging. The person you contact can be across the state or across the country. Messages travel through the Iridium satellite network when cellular/Wi-Fi is unavailable. The device also has check-in and SOS functions.

4. Want ham capability?

Start small, but learn before relying on it

A compact handheld ham radio can receive and communicate over amateur-radio frequencies and repeaters, depending on equipment, location and conditions. A common inexpensive learning radio is the Baofeng UV-5R.

Important: transmitting on amateur frequencies generally requires the appropriate FCC amateur-radio license. Owning the radio is not the useful part. Knowing how to program it, find local repeaters and use it legally is.

5. Keep your phone useful without service

Before a storm or trip, download offline maps for the area you may need. Also save critical addresses, contacts, medical information and meeting locations locally on the phone. GPS can still determine your position without cellular data, but the map itself needs to already be stored on the device.

Easy memory:
NOAA radio = hear what is happening.
FRS radios = talk to your own people nearby.
Satellite messenger = reach people beyond the local outage.
Ham = powerful, but requires learning.
Offline phone + paper = navigation and information when networks disappear.

Before you put the gear away

1. Charge it.
2. Activate anything requiring a subscription.
3. Program contacts/channels.
4. Test it from the places where you expect to use it.
5. Put the instructions and charging cable with the device.
