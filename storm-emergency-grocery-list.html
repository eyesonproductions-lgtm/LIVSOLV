Storm tomorrow? Buy for the outage, not the apocalypse.

What should I get at the grocery store before a storm?

The goal is several days of food and water your household will actually use if the power goes out, with plenty that can be eaten safely without cooking, plus enough cold-storage support to protect what is already in your refrigerator and freezer.

Fast list: drinking water, familiar ready-to-eat food, shelf-stable protein, fruit, crackers or tortillas, shelf-stable milk if your household uses it, a few quick-heat meals, ice or frozen water containers, coffee or comfort items, pet food, medications, a manual can opener and any baby, elder-care or medical-diet supplies your household depends on.

Start with what you already own

Before buying another cart of food, look at the pantry, refrigerator and freezer. Group what you already have into actual meals and decide what should be eaten first if the power fails.

If an outage is likely, freeze water containers with expansion room and freeze refrigerated foods you will not need immediately. More frozen mass helps a freezer hold temperature longer. Put appliance thermometers in the refrigerator and freezer before the storm if you have them.

Prioritize ready-to-eat food. USDA recommends keeping several days of food that does not require cooking or refrigeration. That matters because an outage can take away both your refrigerator and your normal cooking method. Favor foods that need little added water and buy package sizes your household can finish after opening so you do not create leftovers that require refrigeration.

Build actual no-power meals, not just a pile of snacks

Breakfast: cereal with shelf-stable milk, granola with fruit, or peanut butter on bread or tortillas.

Lunch: tuna or chicken with crackers, ready-to-eat beans with tortillas, or nut butter with fruit.

Dinner: canned chili, stew or soup with crackers; a shelf-stable rice pouch with beans or pouched chicken; or another ready-to-eat meal that can be eaten cold if heating is unavailable.

Between meals: nuts, bars, dried fruit, sturdy fresh fruit and familiar snacks.

The point is not to create a perfect emergency menu. It is to make sure the groceries combine into enough recognizable meals that people will actually eat.

Protect the food you already paid for

Buy ice only if you have a plan for it. A hard cooler can protect the refrigerator foods you most care about if the outage lasts. Keep drinks in a separate cooler when possible so the food cooler stays closed.

Block ice or frozen water containers last longer than loose cubes. Frozen potable water also becomes usable water as it melts.

A refrigerator/freezer thermometer is more useful than guessing. Food safety decisions should be based on temperature, not smell or taste.

Know the clock before the outage starts. USDA guidance says an unopened refrigerator keeps food safely cold for about 4 hours. A full unopened freezer holds temperature for about 48 hours, or about 24 hours when half full. Keep the doors closed as much as possible.

What not to waste money on

Do not buy weeks of dehydrated food if you have not solved water. Many emergency meals consume water and fuel.

Do not fill the cart with food nobody normally eats. A rotating pantry is more useful than a shelf of “survival” products your household hates.

Do not overbuy perishables immediately before a likely outage. You are creating another refrigeration problem.

Do not assume every can is shelf-stable. Some canned meat and seafood products are labeled “Keep Refrigerated.” Check the label before counting anything as outage food.

Do not rely entirely on foods that need heat. Your backup cooking plan can fail, fuel can run out, or weather can make outdoor cooking unsafe.

If the power actually goes out

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible. Use refrigerated foods strategically, protect frozen foods, and move to shelf-stable meals as needed. Never taste questionable food to decide whether it is safe.

If flooding occurs, food safety rules become stricter. Do not assume a sealed-looking package is safe after flood-water contact. Follow current public-health guidance before salvaging food.
