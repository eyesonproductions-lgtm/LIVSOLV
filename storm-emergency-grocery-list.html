POWER MAY GO OUT

Just tell me what to get before normal systems stop working.

This is not just a storm list. A tornado, hurricane, ice event, wildfire, equipment failure, grid emergency, planned shutoff or other disruption can leave a household dealing with the same practical problems: no refrigeration, no easy cooking, limited water, weak communications and crowded stores.

One grocery run:

For each person, cover at least three days with water and foods you can eat without refrigeration or complicated cooking. Start with water, peanut butter, crackers or tortillas, canned/pouch protein, beans/chili/soup, fruit, bars, nuts, shelf-stable milk, cereal/oatmeal and foods your household actually likes. Include pet food and a manual can opener.

The simple list

Water: at least 1 gallon per person per day as a basic planning amount, plus pets and household-specific needs.

No-cook protein: nut/seed butter, tuna/chicken pouches, beans and other foods you already tolerate.

Easy carbohydrates: crackers, tortillas, cereal, oats and familiar shelf-stable foods.

Fruit and snacks: canned/packaged fruit, sturdy fresh fruit, nuts, trail mix and bars.

Comfort and kid foods: an emergency is a bad time to discover nobody will eat the shelf-stable food you bought.

Pet food and medications: count animals and household-specific needs from the beginning.

Manual can opener: if the food plan uses cans, make the opener part of the plan.

What real outages add to the shopping list

People who have actually lived through multi-day outages repeatedly report the same secondary shortages: ice disappears, fuel lines grow, pumps may not work, and cooking fuel becomes valuable. A rural outage can also mean the electric well pump stops, so “we have a well” is not the same thing as “we have water without power.”

Useful additions before the rush: freezer ice or frozen water containers, a cooler, adequate safe outdoor cooking fuel for equipment you already know how to use, basic hygiene/toilet supplies, charged lighting, and enough vehicle fuel that you are not forced into a gas line immediately.

Before an expected outage, do these free things

Freeze containers of water. They add thermal mass and become usable water after thawing.

Get the refrigerator and freezer cold now. Group frozen food and minimize door opening.

Freeze food you will not use beforehand. This can buy additional cold-storage time.

Charge everything. Phones, power banks, power stations, radios and rechargeable lights.

Put a thermometer in the refrigerator/freezer. During an outage, measure temperature rather than guessing.

If the power goes out

Do not immediately burn scarce battery or generator fuel just because the lights went out. Keep cold-storage doors closed, assess the likely outage duration, and use temperature measurements. An unopened refrigerator generally protects food for about four hours, while a full freezer can remain cold for roughly 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours under standard food-safety guidance.

Food targets are not starvation thresholds

Human beings may survive periods with much less food than a sensible emergency plan provides. That is not a preparedness target. Plan to preserve strength, judgment, temperature regulation and work capacity. Do not intentionally ration a household toward starvation-level intake because someone else survived it.

The LivSolv rule

If you have $25 and the power may fail tomorrow, solve tomorrow's problem first. If you have the budget and time to build deeper resilience, add layers after the basics work.
