Home readiness without the giant shopping list

The power went out. What do I actually need at home?

You do not need to buy an entire survival store. Start with what you already have, then fill the gaps that actually matter.

Fast answer: Use the safest useful lighting you already have. Preserve the phone battery you already have. Protect the cold already stored in your refrigerator and freezer. Gather the water, food, blankets, chargers, containers, coolers and other useful things already in the house before deciding you need to buy anything.
I have $0 to spend. What can I use around my house?

Start here. This is not a second-rate preparedness plan. It is the first layer.

Light: gather every working flashlight, headlamp, camping lantern and battery light you already own. Check junk drawers for spare batteries. If candles are your only backup, use sturdy holders and the safer-use rules below.

Phone power: fully charge phones, old power banks, laptops and tablets before the storm. A charged laptop can sometimes become a large USB battery for a phone. Put the phone in Low Power Mode, lower screen brightness, turn off unnecessary radios/apps and stop recreational scrolling when preserving communication matters.

Food: eat refrigerated perishables first if the outage becomes prolonged. Keep fridge/freezer doors shut. Group frozen food together. Freeze food-safe containers of water before a predicted outage. If you already own a cooler, have it ready; even inexpensive coolers plus ice can extend cold storage.

Water: before a storm that could affect water service, fill clean food-safe containers you already own. Do not use containers that previously held toxic chemicals. Include water for pets.

Warmth: gather blankets, sleeping bags, warm clothes, hats and socks. Close off unused rooms and concentrate people in a smaller comfortable area when conditions make that appropriate. Never improvise indoor heat with a grill, camp stove, generator or other outdoor combustion equipment.

Cooling: close blinds or curtains against direct sun when that helps, use the coolest safe part of the house, and use natural ventilation when outdoor conditions are cooler and safe. Extreme heat can become dangerous; leaving for a cooled location may be the correct zero-dollar or low-cost solution.

Information: write important phone numbers on paper while the internet still works. Download or screenshot useful emergency information. If you already own a battery radio, dig it out now.

Cooking: first choose foods that need no cooking. Do not move an outdoor grill or camp stove indoors just because the electricity is off.

Sanitation: collect trash bags, soap, hand sanitizer, paper towels, toilet paper, buckets/containers and cleaning supplies you already have. These become surprisingly useful in a longer outage.

Car: charge devices while driving only outdoors, never by running a vehicle in an attached garage. Know how to manually open your garage door before you need to leave.

People: one of the most valuable free resources is another person. Decide now who nearby has power, transportation, tools, medical knowledge, a generator, freezer space, a working radio or simply the ability to check on you, and what you can offer them in return.

Before you buy anything: do the free stuff

Charge everything you already own when severe weather is approaching.

Freeze containers of water if you have freezer space.

Group frozen food together and make the refrigerator/freezer cold before the outage if you have warning.

Put lighting where you can reach it in the dark.

Make a paper contact list and know how to manually open electric doors or gates you may need.

1. Match the light to the job

Headlamp: best when moving or working with both hands.

Flashlight: best for a directed beam.

Battery/rechargeable lantern: better for shared area light.

Solar-rechargeable lantern or flashlight: a useful extra layer for multi-day outages because daylight can extend runtime without consuming disposable batteries. Prefer models that also charge by USB so the tiny built-in solar panel is a backup charging method, not the only one.

Candle: provides dim ambient orientation around the flame but is poor task lighting and introduces fire risk.

Owned and used by LivSolv: We keep an inexpensive unbranded solar + USB rechargeable flashlight sitting in a sunny window. In our actual household use, it has stayed ready when we have needed it and we have never had a problem with it failing to work from that routine. Ours has a main spotlight, a wide COB side light, an integrated solar panel, USB charging input and USB power output. The same physical design is sold under multiple changing marketplace brand names, so we are not pretending one current seller is the manufacturer of ours. We recommend the feature set rather than chasing a particular reseller: USB + solar charging, main beam + COB area light, and preferably USB output. Charge it fully by USB when practical and use the built-in solar panel as additional redundancy.

The little solar-rechargeable things people forget

A large solar generator is not the only way sunlight can reduce your dependence on stored fuel and disposable batteries. Small rechargeable gear can keep low-power jobs running while you reserve the big battery for refrigeration, communications and other important loads.

Solar + USB lantern: excellent for room light. Inflatable solar lanterns are especially easy to store because they pack flat.

Solar + USB flashlight: useful as a secondary light. We favor dual-charge designs because you can start with a full USB charge and still have solar available during a prolonged outage.

Rechargeable AA/AAA batteries: useful for radios, flashlights, sensors and other devices that accept replaceable cells. The more robust setup is quality rechargeable cells plus a USB-powered smart charger, then power that charger from a small solar panel or power bank. Do not assume every gadget advertised as a “solar AA charger” safely charges every battery chemistry.

USB-rechargeable headlamp: hands-free light is disproportionately useful for cooking, repairs, first aid and walking around a dark house. A small solar panel or solar power bank can refill it during a long outage.

Solar-rechargeable radio: a weather radio with USB plus solar and/or hand-crank charging gives you another way to receive information without spending the household's AA supply.

Solar-rechargeable power bank: useful as a supplemental phone-power layer, but the tiny panel on most pocket-size banks charges slowly. For dependable solar phone charging, a separate folding panel feeding a normal USB power bank is generally the more capable architecture.

LivSolv rule for small solar gear: favor products with at least two ways to recharge. Solar is excellent redundancy, but a tiny integrated panel should usually be the backup to USB charging rather than the only charging method.

If candles are what you have, use them as safely as possible

Battery lighting is safer. That does not mean pretending candles cease to exist during an outage.

Use a sturdy holder that will not tip on a stable heat-resistant surface. Keep the flame at least 12 inches from things that can burn. Never leave it unattended or sleep with it burning. Keep it away from children, pets, hair and loose clothing. Do not use candles around home oxygen. Keep working smoke alarms and an appropriate extinguisher accessible.

2. Keep phones alive without buying a giant battery

$0: start with every charged device already in the house and aggressively conserve phone power.

Next step if buying: a quality USB-C power bank may be all you need. For solar redundancy, recharge that bank from a folding USB solar panel rather than expecting a tiny panel glued to a pocket power bank to refill it quickly.

3. Make sure information can still reach the house

$0: preserve phone battery, write down contacts, screenshot/download information, use any existing battery radio, and coordinate with nearby people.

If buying: a battery-backed NOAA alert radio creates a separate information path. A radio that can also recharge by USB plus solar or hand crank adds another layer for a long outage.

4. Protect the food you already paid for

$0: keep doors closed, group frozen food, freeze water ahead of time, use existing coolers and eat vulnerable food in a sensible order.

An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours if the door stays closed.

5. Do not create a carbon monoxide emergency

Never run a portable generator, grill, camp stove or other fuel-burning equipment inside the house, basement, garage or camper. A garage with the door open is still not a safe generator location.

Use working carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup. Never run a vehicle in an attached garage to charge devices.

If you do have money to fill gaps

Buy in the order that solves your actual weaknesses: lighting, phone charging, information, food-temperature monitoring and safety alarms first. Larger batteries, solar and generators come later when your needs justify them.
