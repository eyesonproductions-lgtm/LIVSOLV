Home readiness without the giant shopping list

The power went out. What do I actually need at home?

You do not need to buy an entire survival store. Start with what you already have, then fill the gaps that actually matter: light, phone power, information, food safety and anything your household genuinely cannot go without.

Fast answer: Use the safest useful lighting you already have. Headlamps are excellent for moving and working because they keep both hands free. Flashlights are good for directing light across a room or outside. Lanterns are better for lighting a shared area. Candles are a last-choice light source because they add an open flame, but if candles are what you have, use them deliberately and safely rather than sitting in the dark.

Before you buy anything: do the free stuff

Charge everything you already own when severe weather is approaching: phones, power banks, rechargeable lights, radios and power stations.

Freeze containers of water if you have freezer space. They add cold mass and can later become drinking water if stored in food-safe containers.

Group frozen food together and turn the refrigerator/freezer colder before the outage if you have advance warning.

Put lighting where you can reach it in the dark. A flashlight buried in the garage is not very useful when the lights disappear.

1. Match the light to the job

Headlamp: best when you are moving around, checking a breaker, carrying supplies, cooking or doing anything that needs both hands.

Flashlight: best when you need to throw a concentrated beam down a hallway, across a yard or toward something specific.

Battery or rechargeable lantern: usually the most useful choice for a room. It spreads light around a space instead of making everyone point individual flashlights at what they are doing.

Candle: gives a small pool of warm light around and above the flame. It can provide useful ambient orientation in a dark room, but it is much dimmer than a good LED lantern and poor for tasks that need bright, directed light. The holder and candle itself also block some downward light, so do not expect it to illuminate a work surface evenly.

If candles are what you have, use them as safely as possible

Battery lighting is safer. That does not mean pretending candles cease to exist during an outage. If batteries are dead, a lantern failed, or candles are simply the light source available to you, reduce the risk instead of treating “never use candles” as the whole answer.

Use a sturdy holder that will not tip. Put it on a stable, uncluttered, heat-resistant surface where a person, pet or swinging door cannot knock it over.

Give the flame space. Keep it at least 12 inches from curtains, paper, bedding, clothing, decorations and anything else that can burn. More clearance is sensible when possible.

Stay in the room. Put the candle out when you leave, and never go to sleep with it burning. Bedrooms and other places where someone may fall asleep are particularly bad locations.

Keep hair and loose clothing away while lighting it. Do not burn the candle all the way to the bottom of the holder.

Keep it away from children and pets. Store matches and lighters where children cannot get them.

Do not use a candle around home oxygen.

Have an extinguishing plan. Working smoke alarms matter even more when you introduce an open flame. A suitable fire extinguisher should be accessible, and the adults in the home should know how to use it.

Practical lighting trick: You do not need to brightly illuminate the whole house. Put area light where people are actually gathered, and use a headlamp or flashlight when someone moves through darker parts of the home. That preserves batteries and gives you better light where you need it.

2. Keep phones alive without buying a giant battery

For phones only, start small.

A quality USB-C power bank may be all you need for a short outage. If you want to replenish it from the sun, a separate folding solar panel is generally more useful than a giant-mAh battery with a tiny solar panel built into its case.

3. Make sure information can still reach the house

Your internet connection should not be your only emergency information source.

A battery-backed NOAA alert radio gives you a separate path for official warnings and hazard information. If cell service also fails and you need to communicate with other people, that becomes a different problem.

4. Protect the food you already paid for

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed.

An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about 4 hours, while a full freezer can hold temperature for about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours if the door stays closed. A simple appliance thermometer helps you make decisions based on temperature rather than guessing.

5. Do not create a carbon monoxide emergency

This one matters enough to be blunt: never run a portable generator, grill, camp stove or other fuel-burning equipment inside the house, basement, garage or camper. Portable generators belong outside and well away from windows, doors and vents. A garage with the door open is still not a safe generator location.
Have working carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. When fuel-burning backup equipment is used during outages, a power failure can turn into a poisoning emergency if exhaust enters the home.

Never run a vehicle in an attached garage to charge devices, even with the garage door open.

What should I buy first?

If you are starting from almost nothing:
1. Headlamps and/or flashlights.
2. A battery or rechargeable lantern for shared space.
3. A USB-C phone power bank.
4. A battery-backed emergency alert radio.
5. Spare batteries that match your equipment.
6. Refrigerator/freezer thermometer.
7. Working smoke and CO alarms with battery backup.

Then decide whether your actual needs justify a small power station, larger battery system, solar panels or a generator.
LivSolv rule: Preparedness advice should work in the house you are actually standing in. Safer equipment is worth recommending, but if the ideal item is not available, the useful question becomes: what do you have, what can it realistically do, and how do you use it with the least risk?
