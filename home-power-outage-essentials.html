Home readiness without the giant shopping list

The power went out. What do I actually need at home?

You do not need to buy an entire survival store. Start with light, phone power, information, food safety and whatever your household genuinely cannot go without.

Fast answer: Have a flashlight or headlamp for each person, one good battery lantern for the main room, a charged power bank for phones, a battery-backed way to receive emergency information, working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup, and a refrigerator/freezer thermometer. Then add larger backup power only for the things you actually need to keep running.

Before you buy anything: do the free stuff

Charge everything you already own when severe weather is approaching: phones, power banks, rechargeable lights, radios and power stations.

Freeze containers of water if you have freezer space. They add cold mass and can later become drinking water if stored in food-safe containers.

Group frozen food together and turn the refrigerator/freezer colder before the outage if you have advance warning.

Put flashlights where you can reach them in the dark. A flashlight buried in the garage is not very useful when the lights disappear.

1. Light the house safely

Start with battery-powered light, not candles.

Keep a flashlight or headlamp where each household member can find it in the dark. Add one lantern for a shared room so you are not burning through flashlight batteries just to sit together.

A headlamp is especially useful because it leaves both hands free for checking a breaker, carrying supplies or dealing with damage.

Skip candles as your primary blackout lighting. Federal emergency guidance recommends flashlights and battery-powered lanterns because open flames add a fire hazard at exactly the time emergency response may already be strained.

2. Keep phones alive without buying a giant battery

For phones only, start small.

A quality USB-C power bank may be all you need for a short outage. If you want to replenish it from the sun, a separate folding solar panel is generally more useful than a giant-mAh battery with a tiny solar panel built into its case.

3. Make sure information can still reach the house

Your internet connection should not be your only emergency information source.

A battery-backed NOAA alert radio gives you a separate path for official warnings and hazard information. If cell service also fails and you need to communicate with other people, that becomes a different problem.

4. Protect the food you already paid for

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed.

Federal guidance says an unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about 4 hours, while a full freezer can hold a safe temperature for about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours. A simple appliance thermometer helps you make decisions based on temperature rather than guessing.

5. Do not create a carbon monoxide emergency

This one matters enough to be blunt: never run a portable generator, grill, camp stove or other fuel-burning equipment inside the house, basement, garage or camper. CDC guidance says portable generators should be outside and more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. A garage with the door open is still not a safe generator location.
Have working carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. When fuel-burning backup equipment is used during outages, a power failure can turn into a poisoning emergency if exhaust enters the home.

Never run a vehicle in an attached garage to charge devices, even with the garage door open.

What should I buy first?

If you are starting from almost nothing:
1. Flashlights or headlamps.
2. A battery lantern.
3. A USB-C phone power bank.
4. A battery-backed emergency alert radio.
5. Spare batteries that match your equipment.
6. Refrigerator/freezer thermometer.
7. Working smoke and CO alarms with battery backup.

Only after that should you decide whether you need a small power station, larger battery system, solar panels or a generator.
LivSolv rule: Buy for the failure you are trying to solve. Keeping two phones alive is not the same problem as running a refrigerator for three days. Start with the cheapest layer that actually handles your need.
