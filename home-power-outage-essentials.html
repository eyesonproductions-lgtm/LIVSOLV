Field Guide 01 · Home Power Outage

The power went out. What do I actually need?

You do not need an entire survival store. Protect what you already have, solve the first problems first, then fill the gaps that actually matter.

Fast answer

Get safe lighting. Preserve phone power. Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. Gather water, food, blankets, chargers, coolers and useful equipment already in the house before deciding what you need to buy.

Use what you have first

Start with $0.

This is not the second-rate version of preparedness. It is the first layer.

Light: gather every working flashlight, headlamp, camping lantern and battery light. Check for spare batteries.

Phone power: charge phones, old power banks, laptops and tablets. Use Low Power Mode, lower brightness and stop recreational scrolling if communication may matter later.

Food: keep refrigerator and freezer doors shut. Group frozen food together. If you have warning, freeze food-safe containers of water and ready any coolers you own.

Water: before a disruption that could affect water service, fill clean food-safe containers. Include pets.

Temperature: gather blankets, sleeping bags and warm clothing in cold weather. In heat, block direct sun, use the coolest safe part of the house and leave for a cooled location if conditions become dangerous.

Information: put important numbers on paper, screenshot useful information and locate any battery radio you already own.

Cooking: choose no-cook foods first. Never move an outdoor grill or camp stove indoors.

People: know who nearby has power, transportation, tools, medical knowledge, freezer space or simply the ability to check on you.

Lighting

Match the light to the job.

Headlamp: best when moving or working with both hands.

Flashlight: best for a directed beam.

Lantern: better for shared area light.

Solar + USB light: useful for multi-day redundancy. Prefer dual-charge models so solar is a backup rather than the only charging method.

Candle: poor task lighting and a fire risk. Battery lighting is safer.

Small solar

The rechargeable things people forget.

Solar + USB lantern: excellent for room light, especially pack-flat inflatable models.

Rechargeable AA/AAA: pair quality cells with a USB smart charger, then power that charger from a battery or solar panel.

USB headlamp: disproportionately useful for cooking, repairs, first aid and moving through a dark house.

Solar/USB/hand-crank radio: creates another information path without burning through disposable batteries.

Solar power bank: supplemental only. Tiny built-in panels recharge slowly. A separate folding panel feeding a normal power bank is more capable.

Fire safety

If candles are all you have.

SafetyBattery lighting is safer. If you use candles, put them in sturdy holders on stable heat-resistant surfaces, at least 12 inches from things that burn. Never leave them unattended, sleep with them burning, or use them around home oxygen.
Communication

Keep phones alive without buying a giant battery.

$0: use every charged device already in the house and aggressively conserve phone power.

Next step: a quality USB-C power bank may be enough. For solar redundancy, recharge it from a folding USB solar panel rather than relying on a tiny panel glued to a pocket battery.

Food safety

Protect the food you already paid for.

An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours if the door remains closed.

Critical safety

Do not create a carbon monoxide emergency.

CriticalNever run a portable generator, grill, camp stove or other fuel-burning equipment inside a house, basement, garage or camper. An open garage door does not make a garage a safe generator location. Use working carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup.
