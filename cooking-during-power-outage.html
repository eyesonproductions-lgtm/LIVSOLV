Blackout cooking

The power is out. What do I cook with?

Start with the method that can keep working without stored fuel. Then add a fast backup for cloudy days or nighttime.

Fast answer: If you are buying one emergency cooker specifically for outages, solar deserves the first look. Sunshine is free, there is no fuel to run out of, and there is no combustion exhaust from the cooker itself. Keep propane, butane or a wood-burning option as the backup for nights, storms and heavy cloud cover.

$0 option: build a simple solar oven

You can make a basic box solar cooker from inexpensive or recycled materials. The simplest proven designs use an insulated box, a dark cooking surface, a clear cover that traps heat and a foil reflector aimed toward the sun.

Solar Cookers International publishes open-source box-cooker plans, and the Florida Solar Energy Center has a simple file-box version with a short materials list and step-by-step instructions.

Before you cook: protect the cold food

Refrigerator

An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about four hours. After that, move priority perishables to ice or another safe cold-storage method.

Freezer

A full unopened freezer can hold temperature for about 48 hours, versus about 24 hours when half full.

Want the deeper explanation?

Why not make propane the first recommendation?

Propane is faster, familiar and reliable in bad weather, which makes it an excellent backup. But it is still a finite stored fuel. For a preparedness purchase made specifically for outages, solar adds a cooking method whose energy source is replenished every sunny day. The more cooking you shift to solar, the longer your stored propane or butane lasts.

Solar cooking limitations

Solar cooking depends on adequate sunlight, takes longer than a gas burner and is less convenient at night or during extended cloudy weather. Some foods and cookware fit solar cookers better than others. That is why LivSolv recommends solar first as a fuel-free layer, not solar only.

Carbon monoxide and combustion safety

Fuel-burning equipment can produce carbon monoxide. Never use a generator, charcoal grill, propane grill, rocket stove or outdoor-only camp stove inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, tent or other enclosed or partially enclosed area. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for any appliance specifically approved for indoor use, and keep working CO alarms in the home.

Food-safety details during an outage

Do not taste-test questionable food. Smell and appearance cannot reliably establish safety. Food that still contains ice crystals or remains at 40°F or below can generally be safely refrozen, though quality may decline.
