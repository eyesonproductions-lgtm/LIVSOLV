Blackout food + cooking

The power is out. What do I cook with?

You do not need to compare twenty camp stoves while a storm is coming. Here are the cooking methods that solve different outage problems, the specific models worth considering, and the safety rules that matter.

Fast answer: If you want one simple stored-fuel backup, a two-burner propane camp stove is the easiest place to start. Add a rocket stove if you want a long-outage option that can burn wood or charcoal. Solar cooking can stretch fuel when the sun cooperates. Combustion appliances can create deadly carbon monoxide, so follow the exact manufacturer's location and ventilation rules.

Before cooking: protect the cold food

Refrigerator: think 4 hours

An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about four hours. If the outage will last longer, use coolers and ice to hold priority perishables at 40°F or below.

Freezer: think 48 / 24

A full unopened freezer can hold temperature for about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24. Food that still contains ice crystals or remains at 40°F or below can generally be safely refrozen, though quality may decline.

Do not taste-test questionable food. Smell and appearance cannot reliably establish safety. When in doubt, throw it out.

If you want the simplest answer

Most households: start with propane because it is fast and familiar. Planning for a longer outage: add a rocket stove so cooking does not depend entirely on stored canisters. Want to conserve every bit of stored fuel: add solar as a third layer, not your only cooker.

Propane: easiest all-around blackout cooker

Best value for most homes: Coleman Cascade Classic

Current typical direct price: about $88. Two adjustable burners provide 20,000 total BTU, it fits a 12-inch and 10-inch pan, has wind guards and rotary ignition. Coleman rates burn time at up to about one hour on high on one 16-ounce propane cylinder.

Why this one: two burners make it much more useful for household meals than a tiny backpacking stove without pushing the price into premium territory.

Safety: treat this as an outdoor cooking appliance. Never bring an ordinary propane camp stove into a house, garage, tent, camper or other enclosed or partially enclosed area.

Butane: compact tabletop option

Our butane pick: Iwatani 35FW

The 35FW produces 15,000 BTU, has piezoelectric ignition, a double windbreaker, brass burner and magnetic canister-locking system.

Why this one: if you specifically want a compact single-burner butane stove, this is a well-established model with useful built-in fuel-canister safety features and a real manufacturer manual.

Important: do not interpret “tabletop” as “safe in my house during a blackout.” Iwatani's 35FW manual warns never to use the appliance in an enclosed space such as a camper, tent, car or home because carbon monoxide can kill. The manual's indoor provisions concern properly ventilated commercial restaurant installations, not ordinary residential emergency use.

How to use solar cooking: aim the reflectors according to the manufacturer's instructions, use the supplied cooking vessel/tray, re-aim as needed as the sun moves, and do not assume a solar cooker will work at night or through every weather condition. Solar is best treated as a way to conserve propane, butane, wood or battery capacity rather than the only way you can cook.

$0: use what you already own

Before buying anything, inventory the equipment already in your house. A propane or charcoal grill, grill side burner, compatible gas cooktop, properly installed wood stove, fire pit, manual can opener, pots with lids, thermos, coolers and existing camping equipment may already cover much of the problem.

Do not move an outdoor cooker indoors to make it more convenient. A garage with the door open is not a safe loophole for equipment whose instructions require outdoor use.

Make every unit of fuel do more work

Use a lid

A fitted lid reduces heat loss and usually gets food and water hot faster.

Change the menu

No-cook foods and quick-cooking foods such as couscous, instant potatoes and quick oats preserve finite cooking fuel.

Use retained heat

For suitable recipes, bring food to the required temperature, then use a purpose-built thermal cooker or appropriate retained-heat method to continue cooking without continuously burning fuel.

Use solar when it is free

If you have a solar cooker and good sun, use it during the day and save finite fuel for darkness, clouds and urgent cooking.

If you also need to boil emergency water

Your cooker becomes part of the water-treatment plan. Follow current public-health boiling guidance and use a lidded pot large enough for the amount your household needs. Retained-heat cooking does not replace the prescribed rolling-boil treatment time, and boiling does not make chemically contaminated water safe.

Carbon monoxide is the non-negotiable part

Fuel-burning equipment can produce carbon monoxide. Never use a generator, charcoal grill, propane grill, rocket stove or ordinary outdoor camp stove inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, tent or other enclosed or partially enclosed area. Follow the exact manufacturer's instructions for every appliance.

Keep working CO alarms in the home. If anyone develops symptoms consistent with CO exposure or an alarm sounds, get to fresh air and seek emergency help.
