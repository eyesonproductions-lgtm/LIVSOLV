Small problem. Small solution.

I just want to keep my laptop and phone running.

Then you probably do not need a giant home-backup battery. This is the compact end of emergency power: enough to keep your essential electronics useful without hauling around a 60-pound box.

Fast answer: Phone only? Start with a good USB-C power bank. Want solar too? A power bank that can recharge from a separate small folding solar panel is usually much more useful than one with a tiny panel built into the case. Laptop plus phones? Move up to a compact 245–300Wh power station.

Choose by the problem, not the specs

Phone only

A USB-C PD power bank is the smallest, cheapest answer.

Phone + useful solar recharge

Look for a good USB-C power bank that can accept power from a separate folding solar panel.

Laptop + phones

A compact power station around 250–300Wh gives you substantially more stored energy while remaining easy to carry.

Be careful with giant “solar power banks” sold online. A huge mAh number and a tiny solar panel can look impressive, but that little panel has very little surface area. It may help slowly in an emergency, but it is not the same thing as having enough solar panel to reliably refill the battery. For real solar recharging, we prefer a quality USB-C battery paired with a small folding panel.

LivSolv-owned example: an old Goal Zero Yeti 150

Owned and used by LivSolv.

We still have an older Goal Zero Yeti 150, a small solar-rechargeable power station that predates today's LiFePO4 models. Goal Zero has discontinued it, so we are not telling readers to hunt down a used one. We include it because it is a useful real-world example of this category: a small battery that can keep phones, lights and other low-power essentials running and can be replenished from a compatible solar panel.

The original Yeti 150 used a 168Wh AGM lead-acid battery, two USB outputs, 12V outputs and an 80W continuous AC inverter. Goal Zero specified solar charging through its 8mm input, including roughly 12–24 hours with a Nomad 20 or 5–10 hours with a Nomad/Boulder 50 under its published test assumptions.

What has improved since ours was made: current compact stations are lighter for the energy they store, offer much stronger USB-C and AC output, and commonly use lithium iron phosphate batteries with far longer cycle life. The lesson from our old Yeti is not “buy this discontinued model.” It is that small solar-rechargeable stored power is genuinely useful enough that we still keep ours.

How long will one run a laptop?

Do not trust a single runtime promise because laptops vary enormously. A 288Wh battery does not mean 288Wh reaches your laptop after conversion losses. As a simple example, a laptop averaging 50W for four hours uses about 200Wh. A gaming laptop drawing heavily can empty the same battery much faster.

The number that answers “how long?” is watt-hours (Wh). Think of Wh as the size of the fuel tank. The watt rating tells you how large a load the station can operate at once. LivSolv translates both into actual things you want to run.

Do I need the solar panel?

Not automatically. If you expect electricity to return before the battery is empty, buy the battery first. If you need to keep producing power for days without a working outlet, solar becomes much more valuable.

In this compact power-station class, roughly 100W is a sensible panel size because these stations accept about 100–110W of solar input. Buying a huge panel does not force a small station to accept more solar than its input limit.

One more shopping trap: a “solar generator” is usually a battery/power station plus a compatible solar panel. The panel is not storing power. Check whether the listing actually includes the panel or only the battery.
LivSolv rule: Do not spend $1,500 to solve a $150 problem. Start with the smallest thing that solves what you actually need, then move up only when runtime or the devices you need demand it.
