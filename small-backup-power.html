Small problem. Small solution.

I just want to keep my laptop and phone running.

Then you probably do not need a giant home-backup battery. This is the compact end of emergency power: enough to keep essential electronics useful without hauling around a 60-pound box.

Fast answer: Phone only? Start with a good USB-C power bank. Want solar too? Pair it with a separate folding solar panel rather than depending on a tiny panel built into a battery case. Laptop plus phones? A compact roughly 300Wh power station is the next step.

Choose by the problem, not the specs

Phone only

A USB-C PD power bank is the smallest, cheapest answer.

Laptop + phones

A compact power station around 300Wh gives substantially more stored energy while remaining easy to carry.

Be careful with giant “solar power banks” sold online. A huge mAh number and a tiny solar panel can look impressive, but that little panel has very little surface area. Treat built-in solar as slow redundancy, not your main refill plan.

We still have an older Goal Zero Yeti 150, a small solar-rechargeable station from an earlier generation. It is discontinued, uses older AGM battery chemistry, and is not what we would buy today.

That does not mean we recommend replacing it with another Goal Zero. Current products are substantially better, and we would rather recommend the strongest value system now than stay loyal to the brand we happened to buy years ago.

UPS vs power station: one detail that matters

A normal power station can keep electronics running, but that does not automatically make it a true no-interruption UPS. Some stations offer EPS or UPS-style pass-through operation with a brief transfer time when grid power fails. That is usually fine for routers and many household electronics, but equipment that cannot tolerate even a momentary interruption may need a dedicated UPS.

For ordinary home Wi-Fi: a station with a documented fast transfer time can be very useful. The BLUETTI Elite 30 V2 is rated for UPS switchover in 10ms or less. For critical medical or computer equipment, follow the device manufacturer's backup-power requirements rather than assuming any portable station is suitable.

How long will one run a laptop?

Do not trust a single runtime promise because laptops vary enormously. A roughly 300Wh battery does not mean every watt-hour reaches your laptop after conversion losses. As a simple example, a laptop averaging 50W for four hours uses about 200Wh. A gaming laptop drawing heavily can empty the same battery much faster.

The number that answers “how long?” is watt-hours (Wh). Think of Wh as the size of the fuel tank. The watt rating tells you how large a load the station can operate at once.

Do I need the solar panel?

Not automatically. If you expect electricity to return before the battery is empty, buy the battery first. But if you are building an emergency system specifically because the grid may stay down for days, a real compatible panel makes the setup much more resilient.

One more shopping trap: a “solar generator” is usually a battery/power station plus a compatible solar panel. The panel is not storing power. Check whether the listing actually includes the panel or only the battery.
LivSolv rule: Do not spend $1,500 to solve a $150 problem. Start with the smallest thing that solves what you actually need, then move up only when runtime or your devices demand it.
