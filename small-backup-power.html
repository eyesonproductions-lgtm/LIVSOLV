What will it actually power?

Using conservative planning assumptions, roughly: a 10W Wi-Fi router for about 20–24 hours; a 50W laptop for about 4–5 hours of active use; router + 50W laptop together for about 3.5–4 hours; several phone recharges plus LED lighting with plenty of capacity left. High-draw gaming laptops will run much less.

With the 100W panel: you have a real way to put energy back in during daylight instead of owning a one-shot battery. Solar production varies heavily with clouds, shade, heat, season and panel angle.

We prefer this complete small system over simply recommending another Goal Zero. It is compact, much more capable than our old Yeti, and the panel is part of the recommendation rather than an afterthought.

Why this one beats the obvious alternatives

The current Elite 30 V2 gives 288Wh of LiFePO₄ storage and 600W continuous AC output. We also compared compact Jackery and Goal Zero systems. The point is not brand loyalty. We want the best useful complete system in this size and price class.

Why not automatically buy another Goal Zero?

Our old Yeti is firsthand experience, not a lifetime endorsement of the brand. Current compact competitors can provide more output and better complete solar configurations for the money.

UPS vs power station

A normal power station does not automatically provide uninterrupted switchover. The Elite 30 V2 is rated for UPS switchover in 10ms or less, useful for ordinary Wi-Fi and many electronics. For critical medical or computer equipment, follow the equipment manufacturer's backup-power requirements.

How we estimate runtime

Runtime is roughly usable watt-hours divided by average watts. We deliberately allow for conversion losses rather than dividing the advertised battery capacity by the load and pretending every watt-hour reaches the device. Real runtime changes with device draw, temperature, battery state and whether AC conversion is required.

These are planning estimates, not promises. Measure your actual device when runtime matters. Watt-hours tell you how much energy is stored. The station's output rating tells you whether it can operate the load at all.

Do I need the solar panel?

If this is only for short outages, maybe not. If you are buying it as an emergency system for a grid that could stay down, we prefer buying a compatible real panel with it. Tiny panels built into power banks are slow backup features, not serious daily refill systems.

LivSolv rule: Do not spend $1,500 to solve a $150 problem. Buy the smallest complete system that actually solves the job, then move up only when your loads or runtime demand it.
