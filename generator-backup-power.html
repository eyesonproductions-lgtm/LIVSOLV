Emergency backup power

Gas, propane, dual fuel or solar: which generator should I buy?

Pick the power source that matches the emergency you are planning for. The actual recommendations come first. The technical generator-school stuff is below them.

Fast answer: Dual fuel is our favorite combustion setup because you are not trapped with one fuel. Gas is great when maximum output and easy everyday refueling matter. Propane is attractive when long-term fuel storage matters. Solar generators cost more per watt of output, but they are silent, can run indoors and can keep making power when gas pumps are dead and propane is scarce or expensive. For a serious multi-day plan, fuel + battery + solar is stronger than betting everything on one energy source.

One rule matters more than all the technical details:

Every gasoline or propane generator produces carbon monoxide. Run it outdoors, at least 20 feet from the home and from doors, windows and vents, with exhaust directed away. Never run one in a garage, basement, crawlspace, camper or other enclosed or partly enclosed area. Use working battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms indoors. Battery power stations do not produce combustion exhaust and can be used indoors according to manufacturer instructions.

Now, the generator-school part

Running watts vs starting watts

Running watts are what equipment needs after it is operating. Motors and compressors can briefly need much more power when they start. List the loads you truly need, check each device's running and startup demand, then leave margin rather than operating at the generator's absolute limit.

Gasoline vs propane vs solar in more detail

Gasoline normally gives a combustion generator its highest output. Propane stores far better and avoids stale gasoline sitting in a carburetor. Dual fuel gives you both choices. Solar generators trade fuel dependence for battery-capacity and sunlight dependence. None is universally best, which is why layered systems are so resilient.

How to make fuel and battery power last longer

Do not automatically run a fuel generator 24 hours a day. Many households can run it during higher-demand periods, cool refrigeration and recharge a battery station, then shut it down. With batteries, avoid wasting stored energy on resistance heating and other high-draw loads when lower-energy alternatives exist.

Use a fuel generator to charge batteries

A hybrid setup can be extremely effective. Let the generator handle large loads and rapidly recharge a battery station while it is already running. Then shut the noisy fuel-burning machine down and use the battery for quiet overnight loads such as Wi-Fi, phones, lights and electronics. Solar can keep adding energy whenever conditions allow.

Connecting to the house

Never improvise a connection into household wiring. Powering home circuits requires appropriate transfer equipment and installation. Backfeeding through a receptacle can electrocute utility workers and other people.

Do these free things before buying anything

Walk the house: write down what actually stops working during an outage, including well pump, sump pump, furnace blower, refrigerator, freezer, medical equipment and internet.

Read the labels: record running watts and startup requirements for the loads you care about.

Decide what can take turns: you may not need to run the microwave, freezer and other heavy loads simultaneously.

Check what you already own: extension cords, propane cylinders, power banks, batteries and solar panels may change what generator size you need.

Practice: know where a combustion generator will sit safely, how cords reach the loads and how to start and shut everything down before an emergency.

The system is more than the generator

For fuel generators, plan outdoor-rated cords or transfer equipment, fuel, engine oil, safe weather protection and CO alarms. For solar generators, plan enough compatible panel wattage, correct cables and a safe place to deploy panels in actual sun.

Common failures are mundane: cords too short, missing adapters, stale fuel, dead starter batteries, panels sitting in shade, or buying a battery with plenty of watt-hours but not enough inverter output for the appliance.

LivSolv rule: Do not choose a team. Fuel generators and solar generators solve different failure points. The strongest emergency system uses each where it is good and avoids depending completely on any one fuel, battery or weather condition.
