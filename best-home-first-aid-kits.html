Buy capability, not 300 tiny bandages

Which first-aid kit should I actually buy?

The useful question is not how many pieces are in the bag. It is whether the kit has enough real dressings, gauze, tape, gloves, tools and instructions to handle ordinary injuries, plus room for the household-specific supplies you need.

Fast answer: For most homes, start with an organized reputable kit and upgrade its weak spots. Inexpensive premade kits often pad piece counts while providing too little gauze, too few gloves or supplies the owner does not understand. Training and organization matter as much as buying more gear.

What we would add even if you buy a kit

More sterile 4×4 gauze and larger absorbent dressings.

Good cloth medical tape and roller gauze. Dressings are much more useful when you can secure them.

Extra nonlatex gloves. A couple of pairs disappear quickly.

Quality utility/trauma shears, cold packs, emergency blanket and thermometer.

Eye rinse/saline where appropriate.

Printed first-aid reference. An emergency is a terrible time to discover your instructions require cell service.

Household-specific information and supplies. Medication/allergy list, emergency contacts and clinician-directed personal supplies cannot be standardized into a commercial kit.

Do not let “prepper medical” become cosplay. LivSolv stays focused on recognized first aid and equipment appropriate to the user's training. Bleeding-control equipment such as manufactured tourniquets can be important, but training is part of owning it.
I have $0 to spend. What can I do now?

Put the useful supplies you already own in one clearly marked location. Gather clean bandages/gauze, tape, thermometer, gloves, clean towels, soap, cold-pack capability, scissors, tweezers, flashlight, blankets, current personal medicines and a written medication/allergy/emergency-contact sheet. Then make sure everybody in the household knows where it is.

Organization is a real preparedness upgrade. A useful item scattered somewhere in a bathroom drawer is less useful during an emergency than a modest kit everyone can immediately find.

Don't forget light during first aid

A hands-free rechargeable headlamp belongs near the medical kit. If the grid is down, being able to illuminate an injury while keeping both hands available is more useful than holding a flashlight in your teeth.

For long outages, a USB-rechargeable headlamp can be refilled from a power bank or small solar panel. This is another place where solar redundancy helps without buying a giant power station.

Before buying more gear, buy knowledge

First-aid/CPR/AED and bleeding-control training can add more capability than another pouch of supplies. The kit should include instructions, be checked regularly, and be matched to the people and situations it serves.
