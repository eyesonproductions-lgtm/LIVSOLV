Prepared without packing the whole garage

What should I keep in my car in case I get stranded?

Build for the problems that actually strand people: dead battery, low tire, no phone power, darkness, heat or cold, minor injury and waiting longer than expected for help.

The short list: jump-start capability, tire inflation, hands-free light, reflective warning gear, phone power, safe drinking water, food you will actually eat, first aid and seasonal warmth.

Before you buy anything: check the car

$0 preparedness

Find out what your vehicle actually came with. Locate the spare tire or factory sealant kit, jack and lug wrench. Check that the spare is properly inflated, that the sealant has not expired, and that you have the wheel-lock key if your wheels use locking lug nuts.

Do this in the driveway once, not for the first time on the shoulder of a highway. Your owner's manual shows the vehicle's approved jacking points and tire-change procedure.

An inflator cannot fix every flat. A slow leak may only need air long enough to reach safety, but a puncture may require a proper tire repair or the spare. A plug kit can be useful for someone who knows how to use one, but tread punctures, sidewall damage and larger damage are different problems. Seasonal layer: change the kit when the weather changes

Cold/snow: add a real blanket or sleeping bag, warm hat, gloves, extra socks or warm layers, ice scraper/snow brush, compact shovel and a traction aid such as sand or non-clumping cat litter when snow or ice is realistic where you travel.

Heat: carry substantially more fresh drinking water when traveling in hot weather. Do not rely on the emergency pouches below as your normal supply for a long hot-weather delay.

The point is not to haul winter equipment all year. Adjust the car kit for your climate, season and route.

Best car emergency water
SOS 5-Year Emergency Drinking Water Pouches

These are exactly the kind of foil emergency-water pouches that make sense in a vehicle. Each sealed pouch holds 4.2 oz of triple-purified water, has a five-year shelf life, and the manufacturer rates the pouch for temperatures from -22°F to 149°F. They are U.S. Coast Guard approved and designed specifically for emergency storage.

Why pouches instead of ordinary bottles: cars experience freezing winters and extremely hot interiors. These multi-layer foil pouches are designed for a much wider storage-temperature range than ordinary bottled water and take very little space. Keep several as the reserve you can leave in the vehicle, then bring normal fresh drinking water for everyday use.

Important: temperature tolerance does not mean you should intentionally heat or boil the sealed pouch. It means the unopened package is designed to survive the temperature swings that can happen in a stored emergency kit. Discard any pouch that leaks, swells, punctures or has passed its marked expiration date.

Remote travel: when your phone has no bars

Satellite messaging without jumping straight to Garmin prices
ZOLEO Satellite Communicator

If you drive, camp or travel somewhere cell service can disappear, this is the communication upgrade that matters. Pair ZOLEO with your phone and you can send and receive two-way text, email and app messages through the Iridium satellite network when cellular service and Wi-Fi are unavailable.

What that means if you are stranded: with an active ZOLEO service plan and a clear enough view of the sky, you can still message someone that you are stuck, explain what happened and share your location instead of staring at a phone that says SOS or No Service. ZOLEO also has 24/7 monitored SOS and GPS location sharing.

Your phone does not have to be your only lifeline: the physical ZOLEO device has dedicated SOS and Check-In buttons. Even if your phone is dead or damaged, you can press Check-In to send your chosen contacts an “I'm OK” message with your location, or activate SOS in an emergency. Full two-way messaging uses the paired ZOLEO app.

Know before you buy: satellite messaging requires an active service plan and works best with a clear view of the sky. Terrain, buildings, heavy tree cover and other conditions can affect satellite connectivity.

Keep this in the glove box

Paper still works when electronics do not. Keep emergency contacts, roadside-assistance information, insurance/registration information and a pen where you can reach them. A small amount of emergency cash can also matter if a regional outage takes card readers or ATMs offline.

If you are traveling somewhere unfamiliar, put the paper map here or somewhere equally accessible rather than under everything in the trunk.
