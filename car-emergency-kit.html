What should I keep in my car in case I get stranded?

Build for the boring problems first: dead battery, low tire, no phone power, darkness, heat or cold, minor injury and waiting longer than expected for help. You do not need the most expensive version of every tool.

The short list: a way to deal with a dead battery, tire inflation, flashlight or headlamp, reflective warning gear, phone charging, water, shelf-stable snack, basic first aid, and seasonal protection.

Dead battery: choose by budget, not fear

Best budget jump pack: Schumacher SL1672 1000A Lithium Jump Starter

If you want a self-contained jump pack without spending NOCO money, this is the value choice we would look at first. Schumacher rates the SL1672 at 1,000 peak amps and gives it smart clamps with reverse-hookup protection. It also works as a portable USB power source.

Why buy this instead of the NOCO: price. If it is meaningfully cheaper when you shop and its engine rating fits your vehicle, you are getting the core emergency capability: a charged pack in the car that can start a dead 12V battery without needing another vehicle.

What the lower price does not mean: that it is automatically a worse choice for an ordinary car. Do not pay more simply because the more expensive product exists.

Best overall compact choice: NOCO Boost GB40

NOCO rates the GB40 at 1,000 peak amps for 12V lead-acid batteries, up to 6.0L gas engines and 3.0L diesels. It includes spark-proof and reverse-polarity protection and an IP65 enclosure.

Why we still recommend it when cheaper jump packs exist: the GB40 has an unusually long track record, compact rugged construction, a simple interface and safety features that have been repeatedly evaluated in independent jump-starter testing. You are paying for a proven, compact package and established product history, not for more advertised peak amps.

Who should pay the extra money: someone who values the smaller rugged package and established track record enough to justify the price difference. For an occasional-use emergency pack in an ordinary car, the cheaper Schumacher can be the smarter buy.

Important limitation: neither 1,000A option is the automatic answer for every vehicle. Large gasoline engines, large diesels and unusually demanding cold-weather starts may need a higher-capacity unit. Match the pack to the vehicle rather than buying from a generic “best” label.

The catch with every lithium jump pack: it is only useful if you maintain its charge. Put a recurring reminder on your calendar and follow the manufacturer's storage and charging instructions.

Cheapest dependable architecture: jumper cables

Traditional jumper cables contain no battery and never need charging. The tradeoff is needing another suitable vehicle and knowing how to connect them correctly. Keeping cables alongside a jump pack gives you redundancy.

LivSolv buying rule: when a cheaper product safely solves the same job, we will show it. A more expensive recommendation has to earn the extra cost with a meaningful advantage such as reliability history, build quality, safety design, portability, capacity or a feature you actually need.

Low tire: put an actual inflator in the car

Why this architecture works for an emergency kit: there is no separate inflator battery to remember to charge.

The small renewable-power layer people forget

Solar + USB lantern: USB for normal charging, solar as a slow independent backup.

Solar + USB flashlight: LivSolv has firsthand household experience with this type of flashlight. We keep an unbranded solar/USB rechargeable model in a sunny window and it has remained ready when we have needed it. Because the exact model is sold under multiple names and we cannot verify its manufacturer, we recommend the architecture rather than pretending to identify a specific brand.

What ours visibly includes: an integrated solar panel, USB charging input, USB output/power-bank function, a main directional light and a COB side light. We do not claim an exact battery capacity or solar recharge time because the unit has no identifiable model label.

Rechargeable headlamp: choose USB rechargeable for normal use. A second light accepting replaceable AA/AAA cells gives another independent path.

Rechargeable AA/AAA cells: use quality cells plus a proper USB smart charger. Power that charger from the vehicle, a power bank or a folding solar panel rather than relying on gimmicky direct-solar cells.

Small folding solar panel: useful on remote or longer trips for topping up low-power electronics. It is much more capable than a tiny panel glued to a pocket power bank.

Solar/crank radio: useful when severe weather or remote travel makes a separate information path worthwhile.

LivSolv redundancy rule: small emergency electronics should have two ways to stay alive whenever practical. USB is usually the fast everyday method. Solar, replaceable batteries, vehicle power or a hand crank is the backup.

Then add the cheap stuff

Visibility: reflective triangles or other warning gear.

Light: headlamp plus a second independent light source.

Phone: charging cable plus a dependable backup battery.

Waiting: water, shelf-stable snack and a basic first-aid kit.

Weather: blanket/layers in cold climates, and sun/heat protection where heat is the bigger risk.

Seasonal check: extreme heat and cold are hard on batteries and stored supplies. Inspect the kit periodically rather than assuming something tossed in the trunk five years ago still works.

If the car cannot move

Your kit should help you wait safely, not turn you into a roadside mechanic.

Make yourself visible where it is safe to do so, preserve phone battery, stay aware of traffic and weather, and call appropriate roadside or emergency help.
