When the grid and cell network both fail

How do I communicate from home if a storm knocks everything out?

If the power is out, internet is down and cell towers are overloaded or offline, your phone may stop being enough. The answer is not one magic radio. You need the right tool for the job: hear official information, talk to people nearby, or reach someone beyond the local outage.

Fast answer: At home, start with a NOAA alert radio so you can still receive official warnings. If family members or neighbors are nearby, FRS radios can give you direct local communication without cell towers. If you need to reach someone outside the affected area, a satellite communicator is the strongest backup layer.

For most homes, think in three layers

1. Hear what is happening

NOAA alert radio

This is the first thing I would want in a house during a major storm outage. NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts official warnings and other hazard information independently of your home's internet connection. A S.A.M.E.-capable model can be programmed for your area.

See Midland WR120 →
2. Talk to family nearby

FRS two-way radios

If someone is at another house nearby, outside checking damage, helping a neighbor or separated from the group, FRS radios let you talk directly radio-to-radio. They do not require a cell tower or subscription.

Do not trust the huge range number on the box. Buildings, hills, trees and terrain can shorten range dramatically.

See Motorola T600 →
3. Reach outside the local outage

Satellite communicator

If your entire area has lost cellular service, a satellite communicator can give you a completely different path out. Garmin inReach devices can send and receive messages through the Iridium satellite network and can trigger an interactive SOS with an active subscription and suitable access to the sky.

Portable version for car or go-bag

Emergency radio

A rechargeable portable NOAA radio makes sense when you want something you can move between the house, car and go-bag. It is not a replacement for two-way or satellite communication, but it keeps official information available when your phone is not.

See Midland ER50 →
The easiest way to remember it: weather radio = information coming in. FRS = local people talking to each other. Satellite = a way out when local infrastructure is down.

A practical home setup

You do not need a communications command center. A very practical household setup is one NOAA alert radio that lives in the house, a pair or small set of FRS radios for family/local use, and satellite communication only if your situation justifies the added cost and subscription.

If you live somewhere storm-prone, rural, mountainous or subject to long outages, the satellite layer becomes more compelling because it does not depend on your local internet provider or cell tower surviving the same event.

LivSolv rule: Build communication redundancy the same way you build backup power. Do not rely on one system. Your phone is one layer, not the entire plan.
