When the grid and cell network both fail

How do I communicate from home if a storm knocks everything out?

If the power is out, internet is down and cell towers are overloaded or offline, your phone may stop being enough. The answer is not one magic radio. You need the right tool for the job: hear official information, talk to people nearby, or reach someone beyond the local outage.

Fast answer: Keep a NOAA-capable radio for official information, preferably with a power source that does not depend entirely on the grid. Use FRS radios for direct communication with nearby family or neighbors. Add satellite communication only when you need a way to message beyond the local outage.

1. Hear what is happening

Automatic warning option

S.A.M.E. NOAA alert radio

A dedicated S.A.M.E.-capable weather radio can be programmed for your area and can sound relevant alerts without you continuously listening. Choose a model with battery backup so a blackout does not silence it.

Long-outage option

Hand-crank / solar emergency radio

A self-powered weather radio is valuable when the outage lasts longer than expected. It lets you preserve phone power banks and power-station capacity for devices that cannot be powered another way. Start with the radio charged; use solar or hand crank as an extension and backup rather than assuming the crank provides effortless unlimited power.

Quick reference: what frequency do I use?

If your radio has WX, WB, WEATHER or NOAA mode, select it and find the clearest continuous broadcast. If it displays actual frequencies, NOAA Weather Radio uses seven frequencies in the United States:

162.400 · 162.425 · 162.450 · 162.475 · 162.500 · 162.525 · 162.550 MHz

Test your radio before an emergency and label it with the best primary and backup frequencies for your home. An ordinary AM/FM-only receiver cannot tune the NOAA weather band.

2. Talk directly to nearby family

FRS walkie-talkies: cheap, sweet spot and rugged

FRS radios communicate directly without cell towers, Wi-Fi or a subscription, and compliant FRS operation does not require an individual FCC license. They are useful around the property, between nearby households, while checking storm damage, on road trips and during evacuations.

Ignore the giant advertised range number. Terrain, trees, walls and buildings can cut usable range dramatically. Test the route you actually care about before an emergency.

3. Reach outside the local outage

Satellite communicator

If the entire area has lost cellular service, satellite communication provides a different path out. Garmin inReach devices can send and receive messages through the Iridium satellite network and can provide SOS capability with compatible service. Plans cost money and satellite devices need suitable access to the sky, so this is an added resilience layer rather than a requirement for every household.

Easy way to remember it:
Weather radio = information in.
FRS = nearby people talking directly.
Satellite = messaging beyond the local outage.

Do this before the emergency

Test the NOAA receiver and write the primary and backup frequencies directly on the radio. Pick an FRS family channel and backup channel before anyone separates. Test whether the walkie-talkies actually reach the places you care about. Keep the appropriate spare batteries or charging cable with the radios. If you own a satellite communicator, activate and practice with it before cell service disappears.

$0 option: if you already own compatible FRS radios, use them. There is no preparedness prize for replacing working equipment. Agree on channels, install fresh batteries, test your real-world range and put the radios somewhere everyone can find them.
