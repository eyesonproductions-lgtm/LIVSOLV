When the grid and cell network both fail

How do I communicate from home if a storm knocks everything out?

If the power is out, internet is down and cell towers are overloaded or offline, your phone may stop being enough. The answer is not one magic radio. You need the right tool for the job: hear official information, talk to people nearby, or reach someone beyond the local outage.

Fast answer: Keep a NOAA-capable radio for official information, preferably with a power source that does not depend entirely on the grid. Use FRS radios for direct communication with nearby family or neighbors. Add satellite communication only when you need a way to message beyond the local outage.

Quick reference: what frequency do I use?

If your radio has WX, WB, WEATHER or NOAA mode, select it and find the clearest continuous broadcast. If it displays actual frequencies, NOAA Weather Radio uses seven frequencies in the United States:

162.400 · 162.425 · 162.450 · 162.475 · 162.500 · 162.525 · 162.550 MHz

Test your radio before an emergency and label it with the best primary and backup frequencies for your home. An ordinary AM/FM-only receiver cannot tune the NOAA weather band.

Do this before the emergency

Test the NOAA receiver and write the primary and backup frequencies directly on the radio. Pick an FRS family channel and backup channel before anyone separates. Test whether the walkie-talkies actually reach the places you care about. Keep the appropriate spare batteries or charging cable with the radios. If you own a satellite communicator, activate and practice with it before cell service disappears.

$0 option: use the communications equipment you already own before buying replacements. Learn any satellite features already built into your phone, agree on an out-of-area contact, write important numbers on paper, set family meeting points and test any existing FRS radios.
