How to build a Dakota fire hole

Two separate openings at the surface. One short air passage underground. One small cooking fire.

Fast answer: Dig a larger vertical hole for the fire. A short distance away, on the side the wind is coming FROM, make a smaller opening. From that smaller opening, dig diagonally DOWN THROUGH THE SOIL until it connects near the bottom of the fire hole. The ground between the two openings stays intact above the tunnel.
Outdoor fire only. Do not build this during a burn ban or in peat, duff, root-filled ground, loose collapsing soil or near dry vegetation. Clear the surrounding surface to bare mineral soil.

Understand the shape before you dig

From above: you should see two separate holes with untouched ground between them.

From the side: the smaller hole opens to outside air, then a passage travels through the soil and connects to the lower part of the main fire chamber.

“Upwind” simply means: put the smaller opening on the side the breeze is coming from.

Do not dig an open trench between the two holes. The connecting air passage belongs underground. If you can see the whole connection from above, you dug away too much soil.

Build it step by step

Choose firm, safe soil.
Clear at least several feet around the site to bare mineral soil. Do not use peat, root-filled earth, loose sand or ground where hidden fire could travel.
Dig the main fire chamber.
Make a vertical hole roughly 8–12 inches across and about 10–12 inches deep. Keep it small. This is a cooking fire, not a bonfire.
Make the air intake on the wind-facing side.
A short distance from the main hole, make a smaller surface opening. This opening must remain exposed to outside air.
Connect the intake underground.
From the smaller opening, carefully dig or poke a passage downward through the soil until it reaches the lower part of the main fire chamber. Keep the soil above that passage intact. When finished, you should still see two separate holes from above.
Build a small fire at the bottom of the main hole.
Use dry tinder, kindling and small-diameter fuel. Keep the air-passage opening clear so incoming air can reach the fire.
Support cookware with metal, not sticks.
Use a properly sized metal grill grate or another stable, noncombustible heat-safe support spanning the main opening. Set the pot on top and leave open space around it so hot gases can escape.
Do not jam the pot into the hole. The pot should sit above the main opening on a stable metal support. It should not plug the opening or interfere with the hot gases leaving the fire chamber.
Do not balance boiling cookware on ordinary sticks. They can char, shift or fail. And do not improvise with random wet or porous rocks, which can fracture or spall when heated.

Why the two-hole design works

Fresh air enters the smaller surface opening, travels through the underground passage and reaches the base of the fire. Hot combustion gases rise out of the main chamber. That moving column of hot gas helps draw replacement air through the intake.

Want a reusable stove instead?

A manufactured rocket stove gives you a similar small-wood-fuel option without digging. It is easier to relocate and gives you a purpose-built pot support.

Important fire-safety details

Keep water and extinguishing tools immediately available. Never leave the fire unattended. When finished, extinguish it completely, stir the remains, verify the surrounding soil is cold, then restore the site where appropriate. Follow all local fire restrictions.
