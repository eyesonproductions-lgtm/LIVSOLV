DIY emergency cooking

No stove? Build a simple outdoor cooker.

You do not need to read a survival manual to understand the basic setup. Start with the picture.

Fast answer: If you have suitable bare mineral soil, a Dakota fire hole is the simplest no-metal option. Dig one fire pit, dig a smaller air hole upwind, connect them underground, then build a small fire in the main pit. Outdoor use only.
Do not use this indoors. Solid-fuel fires produce carbon monoxide, smoke, sparks and extreme heat. Do not build one during a burn ban, in peat or root-filled soil, near dry vegetation or where underground fire could spread.

Dakota fire hole: see it first

The whole idea: the fire burns in the larger hole. Wind enters the smaller opening and travels through the connecting tunnel to feed the base of the fire.

The diagram at left comes from a U.S. Army survival field manual and is public domain.

You are making two openings, not one complicated stove.

Build it in six steps

Find safe bare mineral soil.
Clear at least several feet around the area. Avoid roots, peat, mulch, dry grass and anything that can carry fire underground.
Dig the main fire hole.
Think roughly pot-width, not a giant fire pit. The Army diagram shows a main opening around 6–14 inches across and roughly 10–12 inches deep.
Make the smaller air opening upwind.
Place it beside the main pit on the side the breeze is coming from.
Connect the two near the bottom.
Dig diagonally from the air opening into the lower portion of the fire pit. That tunnel is what feeds oxygen to the fire.
Build a small fire in the main hole.
Use dry tinder, kindling and small sticks. You want concentrated cooking heat, not a bonfire.
Support the pot securely and extinguish completely.
Use a stable grate if you have one or bridge firm edges only when the pot cannot tip. When finished, drown and stir the fire and make sure surrounding soil is cold.
Skip random rocks. Wet, porous, cracked or layered rocks can fracture or violently spall when heated. Do not collect rocks from streams or lakes for pot supports.

Want a reusable rocket stove instead?

A rocket stove applies a similar airflow idea in a reusable body: a horizontal fuel feed turns upward into a hot vertical riser. Small dry sticks burn at the end while draft pulls hot gases upward toward the pot.

If you want to fabricate one, keep the combustion path heat-resistant, insulated and reasonably consistent in cross-section, leave room for air around the fuel, and make the pot support extremely stable.

Want the deeper explanation?

Why does the Dakota fire hole work?

The side tunnel gives incoming air a path to the base of the fire. The hot gases rise through the main opening, which helps draw replacement air through the tunnel. The partially sheltered flame can also perform better than an exposed small fire in some windy conditions.

What makes a rocket stove efficient?

Good rocket designs keep the combustion zone hot, preserve a clear air path, burn the ends of relatively small fuel and direct hot gases closely past the cooking vessel. Insulating the hot combustion path reduces heat lost into the stove body.

What are the common DIY mistakes?

Unstable cookware, blocked airflow, stuffing in too much wood, using wet fuel, building over roots or combustible ground, heating wet rocks, using unknown coated or galvanized scrap in very hot zones, and moving the stove indoors because of bad weather.
