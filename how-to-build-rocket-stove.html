How to build a primitive rocket stove

If your ground is full of roots, rocks or hard clay, a Dakota fire hole may be a miserable option. A simple brick or block rocket stove lets you build the same basic idea above ground with common masonry pieces.

The easiest version: Use ordinary bricks, firebricks or concrete blocks to create an L-shaped passage. Feed small sticks into the horizontal opening. The flame and hot gases turn upward through the vertical chimney and concentrate heat beneath a pot.

What can you build it from?

Best: firebrick or clay brick.

Temporary emergency option: ordinary solid bricks or concrete masonry blocks can work for short-term outdoor cooking if they are dry and intact. High heat can crack or spall concrete, so do not treat concrete blocks as a permanent high-temperature stove.

You do not need sheet metal, welding, a grate or a manufactured stove body. The point of this guide is to use masonry you may realistically have around a house, shed, patio, landscaping area or damaged structure.

The basic layout

Think of a capital L lying on its side:

Horizontal leg: this is where the sticks go in and where fresh air enters.

Vertical leg: this is the short chimney that pulls the flame upward beneath the cookware.

The tighter and more enclosed the passage is, the better the draft tends to work. Leave a small gap between the top of the chimney and the bottom of the pot so hot gases can escape around the sides.

A simple no-cut build

1
Choose a bare, noncombustible outdoor surface.

Dirt, gravel or a dry paver base is better than a wooden deck or dry grass. Keep the stove away from buildings, vehicles and vegetation.

2
Make the horizontal burn tunnel.

Lay bricks or blocks so they form a narrow channel with one open end for feeding sticks. You are trying to create a tunnel about the width of a fist or slightly larger, not a giant firebox.

3
Turn that tunnel upward.

Stack more masonry around the far end to create a short vertical chimney. The bend from horizontal to vertical is what gives the stove its L-shape.

4
Start with very small dry fuel.

Use tinder and pencil-size sticks first. Feed longer sticks into the horizontal opening as the fire establishes itself. The stove works best with small fuel and strong airflow, not with a pile of logs.

5
Support the pot above the chimney.

Use stable bricks or another noncombustible support so the pot cannot tip. Leave a gap around the bottom of the cookware. If the pot seals the chimney completely, the fire loses its draft.

OUTDOOR USE ONLY. A rocket stove still produces carbon monoxide, flame, sparks and very hot surfaces. Never use one inside a house, garage, carport, tent, camper or other enclosed or partially enclosed area. Concrete can crack under intense heat, especially if damp. Keep people clear of the stove body and have water or another appropriate extinguishing method nearby.

Why this is useful in an outage

It works where digging doesn't. Rocky soil, roots and hard clay do not matter because the stove sits above ground.

It uses small sticks. You do not need split firewood to boil water or heat a pot.

It concentrates heat. The vertical passage directs the flame at the cookware instead of wasting heat in a broad campfire.

It is easy to dismantle. A dry-stacked emergency stove can be taken apart once you are finished.

Research notes

LivSolv cross-checked simple brick and block rocket-stove designs from preparedness and homestead sources. The consistent principle is a narrow horizontal fuel feed connected to a vertical heat riser, with a gap beneath the cookware for exhaust.

Firebrick or clay brick is preferable for repeated high-heat use. Concrete masonry can be a practical temporary material, but repeated heating can cause cracking or surface spalling.
