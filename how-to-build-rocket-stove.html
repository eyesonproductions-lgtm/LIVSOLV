How to build a Dakota fire hole

Two holes. One underground air passage. One small cooking fire. Here is what that actually means.

Fast answer: Dig a larger vertical hole for the fire. A short distance away, on the side the wind is coming FROM, make a smaller opening. From that smaller opening, dig diagonally DOWN THROUGH THE SOIL until it connects near the bottom of the fire hole. The ground between the two openings stays intact above the tunnel.
Outdoor fire only. Do not build this during a burn ban or in peat, duff, root-filled ground, loose collapsing soil or near dry vegetation. Clear the surrounding surface to bare mineral soil.

The part that is easy to misunderstand: you are not digging an open trench between the holes. The small intake has an opening at the surface. The connecting passage then runs underground through intact soil and enters the lower part of the main fire chamber.
What does “upwind” mean?
Stand outside and notice which direction the breeze is coming from. Put the smaller air-intake opening on that side of the fire hole. The breeze can then enter the intake and help feed the fire. Even in calmer air, rising hot gases help create draft through the passage.

Build it step by step

Choose firm, safe soil.
Clear at least several feet around the site to bare mineral soil. Do not use peat, root-filled earth, loose sand or ground where hidden fire could travel.
Dig the main fire chamber.
Make a vertical hole roughly 8–12 inches across and about 10–12 inches deep. Keep it small. This is a cooking fire, not a bonfire.
Make the air intake on the wind-facing side.
A short distance from the main hole, make a smaller surface opening. This opening must remain exposed to outside air.
Connect the intake underground.
From the intake, carefully dig or poke a diagonal passage downward through the soil until it meets the lower portion of the main fire chamber. Do not remove the soil above this passage. When finished, you should still see two separate holes from above.
Build a small fire at the bottom of the main hole.
Use dry tinder, kindling and small-diameter fuel. Keep the connection from the air passage clear of ash and wood so incoming air can reach the fire.
Use a stable NONCOMBUSTIBLE cooking support.
For a beginner-friendly setup, use a properly sized metal cooking grate or other stable heat-safe metal support spanning the main opening. Set the pot on top. Leave open space so hot gases can escape around the cookware.
Do not jam the pot into the hole. A pot that seals the main opening can interfere with draft and creates an unstable cooking setup. And LivSolv does not recommend balancing a pot of boiling water on ordinary sticks. Use a stable noncombustible support.
Do not improvise with random rocks. Wet, porous, cracked or layered rocks can fracture or spall when heated. Do not collect stream or lakeshore rocks for pot supports.

Why the two-hole design works

Fresh air enters the smaller surface opening, travels through the underground passage and reaches the base of the fire. Hot combustion gases rise out of the main chamber. That moving column of hot gas helps draw replacement air through the intake.

The U.S. Army survival manual describes the same basic construction: a main hole with a large connecting ventilation hole on its upwind side.

Important fire-safety details

Keep water and extinguishing tools immediately available. Never leave the fire unattended. When finished, extinguish it completely, stir the remains, verify the surrounding soil is cold, then restore the site where appropriate. Follow all local fire restrictions.
