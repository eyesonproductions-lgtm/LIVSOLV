WHAT IF SOMEONE GETS HURT?

Build a first-aid setup you can actually use.

You do not need a miniature hospital. You need the ordinary supplies people really reach for, enough gauze and gloves to handle more than one injury, a few smart upgrades, and basic skills.

Do this for free today: Gather the first-aid supplies and medicines you already own into one obvious place. Write down medications, allergies, emergency contacts and important conditions. Check expiration dates. Make sure everyone in the house knows where the kit is.
Call 911 for serious or potentially life-threatening problems. Trouble breathing, loss of consciousness, major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected poisoning, stroke or heart-attack symptoms and other severe symptoms are not DIY medicine. This page is about being better prepared while professional help is on the way or temporarily delayed.

The additions we would make to almost any kit

Wounds

Extra sterile 4×4 gauze

One of the first things a small kit runs out of. Keep a real stack at home, plus rolled gauze and medical tape.

Protection

Nitrile exam gloves

Keep multiple pairs, not the lonely pair tucked into many small kits. They are useful for wound care, bleeding and cleanup.

OWNED + LOVED

Zip-style wound closure strips

These are the clever little adjustable closures that work like a combination of butterfly strips and tiny zip ties. We own them, have actually used them, and love having them in the kit. They can help hold the edges of an appropriate small, clean laceration together without needles.

Important: wound-closure strips are not a substitute for medical evaluation when a cut is deep, contaminated, caused by a bite or puncture, will not stop bleeding, has numbness or loss of movement, or otherwise may need professional closure.

Keep an ordinary medicine layer too

A first-aid kit should not be 200 bandages and zero medicine. For adults, a sensible home layer covers common pain/fever, allergic reactions or itching, stomach trouble and basic minor-wound care. Follow each label, check interactions and contraindications, and keep child medicines/doses separate and age-appropriate.

What else belongs in the everyday layer?

Assorted adhesive bandages, larger absorbent dressings, roller gauze, medical tape, nonlatex gloves, wound-cleaning/irrigation supplies, trauma shears or sturdy scissors, tweezers, thermometer, instant cold packs, elastic wrap and an emergency blanket. Add household-specific medicines, devices and supplies based on the people who actually live there.

Make the kit easy for someone else to use

Keep it obvious. Another person should be able to find it if you are the one who is hurt.

Keep urgent supplies visible. Gloves, shears, large gauze and bleeding-control supplies should not be buried under 100 bandages.

Keep refills nearby. A labeled bin can hold bulk gauze, tape, gloves and dressings while the main kit stays organized.

Keep a medical sheet inside. Include medications and doses, allergies, relevant conditions, emergency contacts and where critical medicines or devices are stored.

Check it twice a year. Replace what was used, damaged or expired.

Prepare for your household, not an imaginary one

Think about prescription medicines, refrigerated medication, mobility equipment, oxygen or powered medical devices, glasses/contacts, hearing-aid batteries, allergy supplies, diabetes supplies, infant needs and pet medications. Make backup plans for medically necessary equipment with the appropriate clinician, pharmacist or equipment supplier before an outage.

If 911 cannot be reached immediately

Try another phone or carrier, text if calls fail, neighbors, radio or satellite communication if available, or physically reach appropriate help when that can be done safely. Continue established first aid that matches your training while help is delayed.

For suspected poisoning in the United States, contact Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 when reachable rather than inducing vomiting or trying a home remedy.
