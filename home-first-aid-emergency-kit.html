Medical readiness at home

If someone gets hurt and help is delayed, what should I have at home?

The goal is not to turn your house into a hospital. It is to have ordinary first-aid supplies, household-specific medications and information organized so you can provide appropriate first aid within your training while professional help is being reached.

Start here: a useful home kit should handle common cuts and scrapes, minor burns, sprains, basic wound covering, temperature checks and cold packs, while also keeping gloves, emergency information and each household member's necessary personal medical supplies together.
Important boundary: LivSolv does not diagnose medical conditions or substitute for a clinician, emergency dispatcher or first-aid training. Serious symptoms, major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, breathing difficulty, loss of consciousness, suspected poisoning and other potentially life-threatening conditions require professional emergency help whenever it is available. If communications are disrupted, use the emergency communication plan and provide only first aid you know how to give.

Build the boring kit first

Wound covering: assorted adhesive bandages, sterile gauze pads, roller gauze and medical tape.

Protection and cleaning: nonlatex disposable gloves, soap/hand sanitizer and appropriate wound-cleaning supplies.

Simple tools: blunt-tip scissors, tweezers, thermometer, instant cold pack and an emergency blanket.

Information: a reputable first-aid manual, emergency contacts, Poison Control information, medication/allergy list and important medical information for household members.

Personal needs: prescribed emergency medicines and routine medications according to the prescribing clinician's and pharmacist's storage instructions. A generic kit cannot anticipate these for you.

I have $0 to spend. What useful things might already be in my house?

Start by gathering what you already own into one known location: clean bandages and gauze, medical tape, a thermometer, disposable gloves, clean towels or cloths, soap, a cold pack or a bag that can hold ice, scissors, tweezers, a flashlight, blankets, prescribed medicines and their instructions, and a written list of medications, allergies and emergency contacts.

A clean towel or cloth can be useful for applying direct pressure to bleeding when commercial gauze is not immediately available. A wrapped bag of ice or frozen food can function as a cold pack for some minor injuries. These are stopgaps, not reasons to improvise invasive treatment or substitute household chemicals for medical products.

The highest-value free preparation may be organizational: put supplies together, write down medical information, make sure everyone knows where the kit is, and learn first aid/CPR from a reputable source.

What should I buy first?

Option 1: assemble your own basic kit

Usually the best value. If you already have some supplies, fill the missing categories rather than paying for a bag full of duplicate tiny bandages.

Pros: tailored to your household, easy to replenish, avoids paying for things you will never use.

Cons: requires a little organization and you must check that important categories are actually covered.

Option 2: a reputable ready-made family kit

Best for convenience. A well-designed commercial kit can give you the core supplies in one organized case. Compare the contents, not the advertised “piece count.” A kit with 200 tiny items is not automatically more capable than a smaller thoughtful kit.

Pros: fast, organized, portable.

Cons: often contains many duplicates and still needs household-specific medications and information added.

LivSolv buying rule: we will recommend specific kits only after comparing their actual contents, useful quantities, refillability, price and independent evidence. “300 pieces” is marketing, not a measure of medical capability.

Prepare for the medical needs specific to your house

A generic first-aid kit is only one layer. Think about what would become difficult during a long outage or storm: prescription medicines, refrigerated medication, mobility equipment, oxygen or powered medical devices, glasses or contacts, infant supplies, allergy medication, diabetes supplies, or other clinician-directed needs.

Do not wait for an emergency to invent backup plans for prescription dosing, medication substitution, oxygen or powered medical equipment. Those plans should come from the appropriate clinician, pharmacist, equipment supplier or emergency authority before the emergency.

When 911 cannot be reached immediately

First, do not assume the medical problem has become a DIY problem. Try alternate communication paths, another phone or carrier, text if calls fail, neighbors, radio/satellite capability if available, or physically reaching appropriate help when that can be done safely.

While help is delayed, use established first-aid procedures that match your training. The American Red Cross framework begins with checking scene safety and the person's responsiveness, breathing and life-threatening bleeding, then getting emergency help and providing care appropriate to the condition and your training.

For poisoning or suspected poisoning in the United States, Poison Control provides specialized guidance. Do not induce vomiting or give a home remedy unless a qualified source specifically instructs you to do so.

The skill may matter more than another gadget

Worth doing: take a recognized first-aid/CPR/AED course and keep a reputable first-aid reference with the kit. Equipment is much more useful when somebody in the house knows what it is for and what not to do.
