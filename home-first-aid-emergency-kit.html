Medical readiness at home

If someone gets hurt and help is delayed, what should I have organized?

The goal is not to turn your house into a hospital. It is to make ordinary first aid, bleeding control, medical information, household-specific supplies and the route to professional help easier to access under stress.

Start here: Put useful first-aid supplies in one known location, keep enough gauze, tape and gloves to handle more than one injury, add a printed medical-information sheet, plan for personal medical needs during an outage, and make sure somebody knows recognized first aid. FEMA includes a first-aid kit in its basic emergency-supply checklist, but the household-specific layer is what makes the kit genuinely useful.
Important boundary: Serious symptoms, major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, breathing difficulty, loss of consciousness, suspected poisoning and other potentially life-threatening conditions require professional emergency help whenever available. If communications are disrupted, use backup communication routes and provide first aid within your training.

Build around the things that actually get used

Wound supplies: assorted adhesive bandages, plenty of sterile 4×4 gauze, larger absorbent dressings, roller gauze and more than one roll of medical tape.

Protection and hygiene: multiple pairs of nonlatex gloves, soap or hand-cleaning supplies, and appropriate wound-irrigation or cleaning supplies.

Simple tools: quality trauma shears or sturdy scissors, tweezers, thermometer, cold packs and an emergency blanket.

Information: printed first-aid reference, emergency contacts, medication/allergy list, pertinent conditions and important medical information.

Personal needs: clinician-directed medicines, devices and supplies stored according to their instructions.

A common kit problem: high piece counts can be padded with tiny bandages while useful consumables such as gauze, tape and gloves run out quickly. Build for capability, not the number printed on the package.

Do not let trauma gear crowd out ordinary medicine

Major-bleeding equipment can matter, but most household first-aid use is much more ordinary: cuts, burns, blisters, sprains, stings, minor eye irritation, fever, stomach problems and medication needs.

A balanced home setup therefore needs both layers: a well-stocked everyday first-aid kit and a clearly organized bleeding-control layer appropriate to the household's training. More exotic equipment is not automatically more useful.

Bleeding control: equipment and training belong together

Direct pressure, absorbent gauze and pressure dressings are foundational supplies. A manufactured tourniquet and hemostatic gauze can add important capability for severe bleeding, but they should be paired with recognized training rather than purchased as decorative “trauma” gear.

STOP THE BLEED training teaches direct pressure, wound packing and tourniquet use. If your household adds those tools, make the hands-on skill part of the purchase.

Organization is a medical capability

Keep the main kit obvious. Another person should be able to find it immediately if you are the one who is hurt.

Stage critical items where you can see them. Gloves, shears, large gauze and bleeding-control supplies should not require emptying the entire kit.

Store bulk refills separately. A home kit can be compact and fast to use while a labeled refill bin holds extra gauze, tape, gloves and dressings.

Check it. Replace used items, damaged packaging and expired products on a schedule. Put the check on the same recurring household date you use for smoke alarms or other emergency gear.

Prepare for the needs specific to your household

Think beyond bandages: prescription medicines, refrigerated medication, mobility equipment, oxygen or powered medical devices, glasses/contacts, hearing-aid batteries, allergy supplies, diabetes supplies, infant needs, pet medications and anything else that would become difficult during a prolonged outage or evacuation.

Do not wait for an emergency to invent a backup plan for prescription dosing, medication substitution, oxygen or powered equipment. Those plans should come from the appropriate clinician, pharmacist, equipment supplier or emergency authority beforehand.

Keep the medical sheet with the kit. Include current medications and doses, allergies, relevant diagnoses, emergency contacts, clinicians/pharmacy, insurance information when useful, and where critical medicines or devices are stored. Update it whenever something changes.

Make a small grab layer

Your large home kit does not need to be your evacuation kit. Keep a smaller pouch or clearly removable module with gloves, gauze, tape, bandages and other essentials you can take quickly. FEMA also recommends thinking beyond a single home kit by keeping smaller emergency supplies in places such as vehicles or workplaces.

Do not split irreplaceable prescription medicines between bags unless that storage plan is medically appropriate. The point is to make basic first aid portable without dismantling the main home setup.

I have $0 to spend. What can I do now?

Gather useful supplies you already own into one known location. Write medications, allergies, emergency contacts and important conditions on paper. Put reputable first-aid instructions where they work without internet access. Make sure household members know where supplies are. Check expiration dates and damaged packaging.

A clean towel or cloth can be useful for direct pressure when commercial gauze is not immediately available. A wrapped cold item can serve as a temporary cold pack for some minor injuries. Improvisation has limits and is not a reason to attempt invasive treatment.

When 911 cannot be reached immediately

Do not assume the problem has become a DIY medical problem. Try another phone/carrier, text if calls fail, neighbors, radio or satellite capability if available, or physically reach appropriate help when that can be done safely.

While help is delayed, use established first-aid procedures that match your training. For suspected poisoning in the United States, contact Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or emergency services when reachable rather than inducing vomiting or trying a home remedy.

The skill may matter more than another gadget

First-aid/CPR/AED and bleeding-control training can add more capability than another pouch of supplies. Keep a reputable printed quick-reference guide with the kit so a dead phone does not remove your instructions.

Practice finding the kit and the medical sheet. In an emergency, somebody other than the person who assembled everything may need to use them.
