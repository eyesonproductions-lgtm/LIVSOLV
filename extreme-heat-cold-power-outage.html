POWER OUT + DANGEROUS TEMPERATURES

I can't heat or cool my whole house. What do I do?

Do not start by trying to make every room comfortable. If staying home is still safe, concentrate people and limited heating or cooling resources in the smallest practical part of the house, reduce heat transfer, and spend your backup energy where it changes comfort or safety the most.

Fast answer: Make a smaller living zone. Close unused rooms. Use doors where you have them. For a large open hallway or doorway, a temporary curtain or barrier can reduce air exchange, but never block a required escape route or create a fire hazard. Then treat cold and heat differently: in cold weather retain heat and safely add warmth; in hot weather block solar heat, increase air movement and move to a cooler location if the house becomes unsafe.

Cold outage: build a warm zone

Choose the zone strategically. A living/family area that can hold everyone, sleeping pads and supplies can work well, especially if it has a properly maintained fireplace. Include access to a bathroom if practical, but don't keep a huge volume of house open just for convenience.

Close what you can. Shut doors to unused rooms. Close curtains/blinds at night, use rolled towels at door gaps, and reduce drafts. In daylight, sun-facing windows can provide useful passive heat, then cover them again as temperatures fall.

No door across a wide opening? A tension-mounted insulated curtain, moving blanket or other temporary barrier can help create a smaller zone. Heavy plastic can reduce drafts but provides little insulation by itself, so a fabric/insulating layer is usually more useful. Keep all temporary material well away from fireplaces, heaters, candles and other ignition sources and preserve clear exits.

Heat people before trying to heat cubic feet. Layers, hats, socks, sleeping bags and blankets require no electricity. If limited battery power is available, targeted personal warming can sometimes use far less energy than an electric space heater.

Products worth comparing for cold outages

Insulated doorway curtains / tension rods: useful for temporarily dividing a large open plan without permanent construction.

Window insulation and thermal curtains: reduce heat loss and remain useful after the emergency.

Low-watt heated blankets or throws: potentially much easier on a power station than trying to run a 1,500-watt space heater. Match the actual wattage and plug type to your backup-power system.

Sleeping bags and insulated pads: useful even with zero electrical power.

Battery CO alarms and smoke alarms: essential safety equipment around combustion heat.

If you have a fireplace

A code-compliant, maintained fireplace with dry fuel can be a legitimate outage heat source. Use a glass or metal screen, keep combustible temporary room dividers and bedding well away from it, and never leave the fire unattended. Have the chimney/fireplace inspected and maintained before you need it.

Never use an oven, range, grill, camp stove or outdoor heater as an improvised indoor heating system. Generators stay outdoors away from doors, windows and vents.

Hot outage: create the coolest zone you can

Stop heat before fighting it. Close blinds/curtains on sun-exposed windows. Pick the coolest safe part of the home, often a lower level or shaded side, and concentrate there.

Air movement is high-value. Rechargeable fans and efficient DC/USB fans can provide substantial comfort for much less battery demand than air conditioning. If you have a power station, this is one of the first loads worth considering.

Ventilate strategically. When outdoor air is cooler than indoor air and conditions are safe, use cross-ventilation. When outside is hotter, especially in direct sun, opening everything can simply import more heat.

About swamp coolers: evaporative cooling works best in dry air. In humid climates it can add humidity while providing disappointing cooling, so LivSolv would not treat a small evaporative cooler as a universal outage solution.

Products worth comparing for hot outages

Rechargeable camping/utility fans: long-runtime airflow without running an inverter continuously.

USB-C/DC fans: pair efficiently with power banks or small power stations.

Blackout/solar-blocking window coverings: particularly useful on high-sun windows.

Power stations sized for fans: a much smaller and cheaper problem than sizing backup power for central AC.

Portable/window AC: a higher-power tier. If extreme heat is a recurring outage risk, this deserves a dedicated sizing guide rather than assuming any portable battery will run it for long.

I have $0 to spend. What can I do with what I already own?

Cold: close unused rooms, put towels at drafts, close curtains at night, use blankets/sleeping bags/warm clothing, move mattresses into one smaller living zone, use sun through windows during the day when helpful, and coordinate with family or neighbors who have safe heat.

Heat: close sun-facing blinds/curtains, move everyone to the coolest shaded or lower part of the house, use any battery fans you already own, conserve phone/power-bank energy, use safe natural ventilation when outdoors is cooler, and identify a cooled location you can relocate to before indoor temperatures become dangerous.

The point where you leave

A smaller zone can stretch limited resources. It cannot make every temperature safe. The American Red Cross specifically advises planning to relocate to a heated or air-conditioned location when a home becomes too hot or too cold. Infants, older adults and people with certain medical conditions can be particularly vulnerable.

LivSolv's goal is not to encourage people to remain in a dangerous house because they bought the right curtain or fan. Build a stay-home plan and a leave-home threshold.
