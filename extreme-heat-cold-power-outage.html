POWER OUT + DANGEROUS TEMPERATURES

I can't heat or cool my whole house. What do I do?

The practical preparedness answer is usually not “replace your HVAC with batteries.” Build a smaller microclimate, protect people first, and use limited fuel or stored electricity where it does the most work.

Fast answer: Pick a survivable zone instead of the whole house. In winter, shrink it again if necessary: room → sleeping area → even a tent inside the room. In summer, block the sun before the house heats up, retreat to the coolest shaded/lower area, cool people directly, and use limited electricity for efficient airflow before attempting air conditioning.

Cold outage: the warm-room strategy

Choose the room, not just the heater. Practical preparedness guides repeatedly recommend putting everyone in one room. A space with a safe fireplace or wood stove can outweigh the theoretical advantage of a smaller room. Otherwise favor a room that can be closed off, fits everyone and sleeping gear, and has limited exterior exposure. Southern sun can provide passive heat during the day.

Your open hallway idea is legitimate. If the useful living area includes the kitchen, living room and family room but opens into the rest of the house, isolate that zone. A tension rod plus heavy curtain, moving blankets or layered temporary barrier can reduce air exchange. Painter's plastic and tape are useful for drafts and window leakage, but plastic alone is not much insulation. Never obstruct a needed exit and keep all barriers well away from flame and heat sources.

Use daylight like free heat. Let direct winter sun through useful sun-facing windows during the day, then cover those windows as temperatures fall. Consumer Reports and preparedness guides both recommend this passive-solar approach.

Stop the small leaks. Towels or rolled blankets at door bottoms, closed drapes, temporary window plastic, weatherstripping you already own and blankets over particularly cold windows can materially reduce drafts.

The prepper trick worth adding: a tent inside the house

If the house or even your chosen room becomes very cold, several preparedness sources recommend pitching a normal camping tent inside the warm room and sleeping in it. You are no longer trying to keep hundreds of square feet comfortable. You are creating a tiny microclimate around sleeping people.

Sleeping bags and insulated pads go inside. Some preparedness guides suggest draping light blankets over the tent to reduce heat loss further, but do not obstruct ventilation and never put a flame, combustion heater, candle or other ignition source inside or against a tent.

This is exactly the kind of $0/use-what-you-own tactic LivSolv should surface because many families already have a camping tent, air mattresses, comforters and sleeping bags.

What is actually worth buying for cold outages?

1. Insulated doorway curtain + tension rod: unusually useful in open-plan houses because it lets you temporarily abandon part of the floor plan.

2. Window insulation film / clear plastic: cheap draft and heat-loss reduction while still allowing daytime solar gain.

3. Good sleeping bags + insulated pads: insulation underneath matters because a cold floor can pull heat away from you.

4. Low-watt heated blanket/throw: targeted warmth can be dramatically easier to power than a 1,500W space heater. We should compare actual wattage, not marketing.

5. Battery CO + smoke alarms: non-negotiable around fireplaces or other permitted combustion heat.

6. Power station: prioritize targeted electric warmth, communications and other critical loads before assuming it can economically heat a large room all night.

If you have a fireplace

A maintained, code-compliant fireplace with dry fuel can anchor the warm zone. Keep bedding, plastic, curtains and temporary dividers far from it, use the appropriate screen, maintain working smoke/CO alarms, and follow the fireplace manufacturer's and local fire-safety requirements.

Do not turn an oven, grill, camp stove, generator or outdoor heater into an improvised indoor heater. Some portable fuel-burning heaters are specifically designed and labeled for indoor use, but that is a product-specific safety category we should evaluate separately rather than casually telling readers to burn propane indoors.

Hot outage: fight the sun first, then cool the people

Do not wait until the room is roasting to close the shades. Prepper heat guides emphasize blocking direct solar gain early. Cover the hottest sun-facing windows before peak heat.

Use the house's natural temperature gradient. A shaded ground-floor room or basement is often the better daytime refuge. Move people, pets, water, medications and charging gear there instead of repeatedly moving through the hot house.

Night-flush the house when conditions allow. If outdoor air becomes meaningfully cooler than indoor air, open strategically and create cross-flow. A fan exhausting hot air from one opening while cooler air enters another can accelerate the exchange. Then close the house and block sun again before outdoor temperatures climb. Do not do this when outdoor smoke/air quality makes ventilation unsafe.

Cool bodies, not just cubic feet. Air moving across damp skin can provide much more benefit per watt than trying to refrigerate the room. Damp cloths/cooling towels, appropriate light clothing, hydration and reducing physical activity are low-energy tools.

Fan caveat: fans move air; they do not lower room temperature. In dangerously hot indoor conditions, a fan is not a substitute for air conditioning or relocation.

What about the DIY “swamp cooler” idea?

The classic bucket/cooler + ice/water + fan trick can create a localized cool breeze, but it is not a magic room air conditioner. Evaporative cooling works far better in dry climates. In humid weather it can add moisture with limited temperature reduction. Ice-based DIY coolers also consume stored ice, which may be more valuable for food or medical needs.

LivSolv should treat these as situational personal-cooling tricks, not promise that a homemade swamp cooler will make a hot humid house safe.

What is actually worth buying for hot outages?

1. Rechargeable high-runtime fan: probably the first dedicated blackout-cooling purchase for many homes.

2. Efficient USB-C/DC fans: let ordinary power banks become part of the cooling plan without wasting energy through an AC inverter.

3. Solar-blocking/thermal window coverings: prevention is cheaper in watts than removing heat after it enters.

4. Cooling towels + simple personal cooling gear: inexpensive and almost no energy demand.

5. Power station sized for fans: substantially easier and cheaper than backing up air conditioning.

6. Efficient window/portable AC + correctly sized battery: the higher-capability tier for households where extreme heat is a serious recurring risk. This needs its own runtime calculator and buying guide.

I have $0 to spend. What can I do with what I already own?

Cold: pick one room, close the rest, hang blankets/curtains across unused openings, block drafts with towels, use clear plastic you already have on leaky windows, bring mattresses and bedding together, wear layers/hats/socks, use daytime sun, and pitch a camping tent indoors as a smaller sleeping microclimate if needed.

Heat: block sun before peak heat, move downstairs or to the shadiest room, avoid adding heat with ovens/dryers, use existing battery/USB fans, damp cloths and safe body-cooling methods, open for cooler night air when appropriate, then close back up before the day heats up. Know where you can go for actual air conditioning if the house becomes unsafe.

The point where you leave

Preparedness is not proving you can endure anything inside your house. A microclimate buys margin. If indoor temperatures become unsafe, especially for infants, older adults, people with medical vulnerabilities or pets, the plan changes from “make the room better” to “get somewhere safely heated or cooled.”
