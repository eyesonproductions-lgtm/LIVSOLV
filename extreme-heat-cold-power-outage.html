POWER OUT + DANGEROUS TEMPERATURES

I can't heat or cool my whole house. What do I do?

Protect people, not square footage. Start with the free things that reduce heat loss or heat gain, then use limited battery power where it changes how a person actually feels.

Fast answer: Cold: close down to one room, stop drafts and warm bodies and beds instead of the whole house. Heat: block sun early, move to the coolest part of the building, cool skin with water, reduce activity and use battery fans only while they are actually helping. If the house becomes dangerous, the plan changes from “stay comfortable” to “get people somewhere safer.”

Cold outage: make one room easier to keep warm

Free first: close unused rooms, block door-bottom drafts with towels, use blankets as safe temporary dividers, open sun-facing curtains during useful winter sun and close them again before temperatures fall. Bring people and bedding into the same room.

If it becomes very cold, a camping tent pitched indoors can make a smaller sleeping microclimate. Use insulated bedding underneath you too. Never put a candle, combustion heater or flame inside or against the tent.

Hot outage: what if there really is no AC anywhere?

First, stop adding heat. Block direct sun before the room heats up, avoid ovens and other heat-producing appliances, move to a shaded lower floor or basement when it is cooler, and use cooler night air when outdoor temperature and air quality make that helpful.

Then cool the body directly. Take a cool shower or bath if water is available. Wet skin or clothing, use damp cloths or a cooling towel on the neck and body, drink water, reduce physical activity and wear loose lightweight clothing.

About the CDC 90°F fan warning: CDC currently says fans should be used for personal cooling when indoor temperature is below 90°F because at higher indoor temperatures a fan can increase body temperature. That does not mean “do nothing if the grid is down.” Above that point, shift the emphasis to water-based body cooling, shade, the coolest available part of the structure, hydration and leaving the hot structure if a safer location can be reached. A cooling center, library, store, vehicle with functioning AC, generator-powered community facility or an unaffected neighboring area may still have cooling even during a local outage.

I have $0 to spend. Give me only the free plan.

Cold: close unused rooms, block drafts, gather bedding and people, layer clothing, use useful daytime sun and create a smaller sleeping space.

Heat: block sun early, move downstairs or to the coolest shaded room, stop cooking indoors, take cool showers/baths, wet skin or clothing, use damp cloths, hydrate, reduce activity, purge heat with cooler night air when safe, and leave for a safer location if the building becomes dangerously hot.

Know when staying is no longer the plan

Heat illness and hypothermia are medical problems, not endurance tests. If someone develops confusion, fainting, severe weakness or other signs of serious heat or cold illness, seek emergency help. If the structure itself is becoming unsafe, use the communication and evacuation plan rather than spending the last of your battery trying to make an unlivable room comfortable.
