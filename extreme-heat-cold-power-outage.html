POWER OUT + DANGEROUS TEMPERATURES

I can't heat or cool my whole house. What do I do?

Protect people, not square footage. Start with free ways to reduce heat loss or heat gain, then spend battery power where it changes how a person actually feels.

Fast answer: In cold weather, close down to one room, stop drafts and warm bodies and beds instead of the whole house. In heat, block sun early, move to the coolest part of the building, cool skin with water and reduce activity. Fans help in the right temperature range, but they are not air conditioners. If the building becomes dangerous, leave for a safer location.

Cold outage: shrink the space

Free first: close unused rooms, block door-bottom drafts with towels, open sun-facing curtains during useful winter sun and close them before temperatures fall. Bring people and bedding into one room. An indoor camping tent can create a smaller sleeping microclimate. Insulate underneath you as well as on top.

Never use grills, camp stoves, generators or other outdoor combustion equipment indoors. Never put a flame or combustion heater inside a tent.

Hot outage: cool people first

Stop adding heat. Block direct sun before the room heats up. Avoid ovens and heat-producing appliances. If outdoor air is hotter than indoor air, keep windows closed; when outside becomes cooler, use that cooler air to purge heat.

Cool the body. Take cool showers or baths, wet skin or clothing, use damp cloths or a cooling towel, drink water and reduce activity.

Fan warning: CDC currently advises using fans for personal cooling when indoor temperatures are below 90°F. At higher temperatures a fan can increase body temperature. Above that point, emphasize water-based body cooling and getting to an air-conditioned or otherwise safer location rather than relying on airflow alone.

Dry climate? Evaporative cooling can work

First, the big limitation: swamp coolers work by evaporating water. They can work very well in hot, dry air, but their cooling ability drops sharply as humidity rises. In humid parts of the country, including much of the Southeast, they are not a substitute for air conditioning.

Skip the tiny desktop “air conditioner” gadgets. We've tried small personal swamp-cooler-style fans and have not found them worth the money. A little fan blowing across a tiny wet filter may feel slightly cooler right in front of your face in dry air, but it is not going to meaningfully cool a hot room. Portable evaporative coolers also need airflow to the outside so they do not simply keep adding humidity to the same enclosed air.

I have $0 to spend

Cold: close unused rooms, block drafts, gather bedding and people, layer clothing, use useful daytime sun and create a smaller sleeping space.

Heat: block sun early, move to the coolest shaded area, stop cooking indoors, take cool showers or baths, wet skin or clothing, hydrate, reduce activity, use cooler night air when safe, and leave for a safer location if the building becomes dangerously hot.

Know when staying is no longer the plan

Heat illness and hypothermia are medical emergencies, not endurance tests. Confusion, fainting or severe deterioration means seek emergency help. If the structure is becoming unsafe, switch to the communication and evacuation plan.
