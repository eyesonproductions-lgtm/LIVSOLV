Power out + dangerous temperatures

I can't heat or cool my whole house. What do I do?

Do not try to recreate whole-house HVAC. Make one smaller area survivable and concentrate your power, insulation and supplies on the people in it.

Fast answer: If it is cold, make one warm room and warm bodies and beds. If it is hot, make one cool zone, block the sun and cool people directly. If you cannot keep that space safe, the solution is to relocate to somewhere with working heat or air conditioning.

Cold outage: build one warm room

Do this first. Pick the smallest practical occupied room. Close doors, block drafts, gather everyone there, layer dry clothing and hats, and move mattresses, sleeping bags and blankets into the space. Insulation underneath you matters too.

Insulated doorway curtain + window shrink film

Reduce the space you are losing heat from. The curtain can close an open passage without construction. Window film helps cut drafts and heat loss through leaky windows.

Do not improvise indoor heat with combustion. Never heat the house with a gas oven. Never use a grill, camp stove or generator indoors or in a garage. If you use a fireplace, wood stove or other approved heater, use it only as designed and keep working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms. Portable generators belong outside, more than 20 feet from doors, windows and vents.
Leave if you cannot keep people warm enough. Shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, slurred speech and unusual drowsiness can signal hypothermia. A body temperature below 95°F is a medical emergency.

Hot outage: build one cool zone

Do this first. Block direct sun before rooms heat up, stop using ovens and other heat-producing appliances, move to the coolest part of the building, reduce activity, drink water and cool skin with cool showers, baths or wet cloths. Use cooler outdoor air when it actually becomes cooler outside.

Fans have a limit. Current CDC guidance says to use fans indoors when temperatures are below 90°F. Above 90°F, a fan can increase body temperature. At that point, use water-based body cooling and get to an air-conditioned or otherwise safer place rather than relying on airflow alone.
What about a portable swamp cooler? Evaporative coolers can be useful in genuinely hot, dry climates, but lose effectiveness as humidity rises. They are not a reliable substitute for air conditioning in humid climates. We are keeping that as optional deeper information rather than making it part of the basic outage kit.

Leave if the space is becoming dangerously hot. Confusion, fainting, worsening heat illness or inability to cool a vulnerable person means the plan changes from “stay comfortable” to “get somewhere cooler.” Libraries, malls and community cooling locations can be useful when available.

I have $0 to spend

Cold: shrink to one room, block drafts with towels, gather bedding, wear dry layers and hats, insulate underneath sleeping areas and use useful daytime sun.

Heat: block sun early, move downstairs or to the coolest shaded area, stop cooking indoors, cool skin with water, hydrate, reduce activity and use cooler night air when conditions allow.

Sources and safety notes
